%%               ABSTRACT              %%

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We study the long-term effects of Mexico's \textit{Progresa} conditional cash transfer (CCT) program on adult mortality at ages 65 and older. Using vital statistics combined with administrative records on program enrollment, and a difference-in-differences design that exploits geographic variation in program intensity across highly marginalized municipalities, we do not find robust evidence of medium- or long-run mortality reductions. We show that previously reported short-run mortality reductions are sensitive to weighting and lag specifications, becoming smaller and statistically insignificant under equally defensible specifications. To examine behavioral channels, we use \textit{Progresa}'s own experimental evaluation data and find no significant effects on older adults' labor supply or household composition. We conclude that the evidence does not support the claim that Progresa's household-based conditional cash transfer program delivered mortality benefits to older adults of the magnitude previously reported.

\noindent \\
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Mortality, Conditional Cash Transfers, \textit{Progresa}, Mexico, Older Adults \\
\noindent\textbf{JEL Codes:} I13, I14, I15, I18
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{s:intro}

\p Older adults have become one of the fastest-growing population groups worldwide as fertility has declined across both developed and developing countries \citep{UN2024WPP}.\footnote{Sustained declines in fertility rates shrink the share of younger cohorts entering the population while life expectancy gains keep older cohorts alive longer.} In Latin America and the Caribbean, the share of the population aged 65 and older stood at 9.9\% in 2024 and is projected to nearly double to 18.9\% by 2050 \citep{UN2024WPP}, with a growing share of this population concentrated in low- and middle-income countries \citep{WHO2024Ageing}. Yet population aging in the region is unfolding against a backdrop of persistent material deprivation: according to the most recent estimates, one in five older adults in Latin America lives in multidimensional poverty, facing simultaneous deficits in income, healthcare access, and social support \citep{CEPAL2024SocialPanorama}.\footnote{By comparison, adults aged 65 and older represent approximately 18\% of the population in OECD countries on average \citep{OECD2024Society}, yet their income poverty rate—measured as the share living below half the national median income—stands at around 15\% \citep{OECD2024Society}} Therefore, understanding the levers through which income can improve the welfare of older adults remains unclear.

%—and especially income that reaches older adult individuals indirectly, through other household members—

\p The health effects of income-support programs for older adults have attracted substantial attention, yet the evidence remains mixed. Some studies find important health benefits \citep{ Case2004PerspectivesAging, BarhamRowberry2013JDE, Aguila2015PNAS, MiglinoNavarrete2023AEJP, Menares2026JHR}, others find no effects \citep{LloydSherlockAgrawal2014JDS}, and still others identify unintended adverse consequences linked to obesity and diet change \citep{FernaldGertlerHou2008JN, KronebuschDamon2019EHB}. Most existing evidence focuses on short-run outcomes, leaving long-run mortality effects poorly understood.

\p In this paper, we leverage the Mexico \textit{Progresa} conditional cash transfer (CCT) program, launched in 1997 and subsequently renamed Oportunidades and then Prospera, that offers the opportunity to study longer-term mortality effects of a large-scale transfer program. \textit{Progresa} has served as a model for CCT programs in over 60 countries \citep{Fiszbein2009CCTBook, Baird2014JDEffectiveness, ParkerTodd2017JEL}, and a growing literature evaluates its health impacts \citep{Gertler2004AER, LagardeHainesPalmer2009Cochrane, RiveraHernandez2016HSR, ParkerTodd2017JEL}. However, long-term effects—particularly on mortality among aging adults—remain largely unstudied \citep{ParkerTodd2017JEL}.


\p Whether a child-focused CCT can generate mortality benefits for older adults raises a natural question: through what channels could a CCT designed for women with young children plausibly affect older adults' health? Three pathways are grounded in the program's design, and provide a plausible theoretical basis for expecting \textit{Progresa} to benefit older adults household members indirectly. First, the annual health check-up conditionality requires \textit{all} household members—including older adults—to attend one annual clinic visit, creating an opportunity for early detection and treatment of conditions such as infectious disease, diabetes, and hypertension that disproportionately drive mortality in older age \citep{Angel2017Gerontologist, aguila2024conditional}. Second, the cash transfer raises household income by over 25\% \citep{Gertler2004AER, ParkerTodd2017JEL}, potentially improving nutrition and healthcare access for older adult members who share household resources. Third, if program-induced changes in labor or living arrangements alter the availability of informal caregiving within the household, this could affect older adult's health through established social-support pathways \citep{duflo2000child, BehrmanParker2013Demography}. 

\p The effects of Progresa on older adults' household members have focused on narrow outcomes and are almost entirely short-run. \cite{BehrmanParker2013Demography} document improved health-service utilization for older adults women but not men six years after implementation. \cite{aguila2024conditional} find that \textit{Progresa} improved hypertension diagnosis and treatment use among adults aged 50 and older but did not reduce uncontrolled blood pressure as measured by biomarkers, suggesting that CCTs can raise healthcare utilization without producing clinically meaningful improvements in chronic disease control. \cite{BarhamRowberry2013JDE} reported short-run reductions in aggregate older adults' mortality over the first five years of program operation, but no study has examined whether such effects persist or accumulate over a longer horizon. 

% By contrast, evidence on programs that target older adults directly, such as Mexico's subsequent \textit{70 y Más} non-contributory pension reduced older adults women mortality in rural areas \citep{Menares2026JHR} by 5.5\%. 

%, and \cite{MiglinoNavarrete2023AEJP} find that Chile's non-contributory basic pension reduced four-year mortality by 2.7 percentage points among older adults recipients. 


\p In this paper, following \cite{parker2023conditional}, we focus on highly marginalized municipalities, where the program was most heavily targeted, and estimate difference-in-differences models exploiting variation in \textit{Progresa} enrollment intensity across municipalities that enrolled more intensively in the program's early phase (1997--1999), to study mortality between 1991 and 2006. Previous literature, such as \cite{BarhamRowberry2013JDE}, relied on time-varying treatment intensity, but recent developments in difference-in-differences methods show that this approach can produce forbidden comparisons, in which municipalities whose enrollment has already stabilized are implicitly used as controls for municipalities still increasing their enrollment.\footnote{Recent work by \cite{deChaisemartinDHaultfoeuille2024IntertemporalDiD} develops a continuous-treatment estimator that avoids this problem by comparing municipalities whose intensity changes from year to year ("switchers") to municipalities whose intensity is flat ("stayers"). In our setting, treatment intensity keeps rising across nearly all municipalities throughout the study period, leaving few stayers to serve as valid controls.} In our design, treatment timing is common across all municipalities, so we never compare already-treated to newly-treated units, preventing forbidden comparisons. Separately, our specification controls for total cumulative enrollment through 2005 to avoid comparing municipalities that would have ended with different total enrollment, reflecting characteristics that could themselves be correlated with mortality. Fixing intensity at a single point per municipality also lets us interact it with year indicators and test directly for pre-trends.

\p Our main finding is that we do not find statistically significant effects of \textit{Progresa} on older adults' mortality during its first ten years. This null result is robust to weighting, to different set of socioeconomic trends, for Seguro Popular (a health insurance program for informal-sector workers implemented in 2002), and to alternative outcome measures (levels, logs, counts, and age adjustments). We extend our analysis to cause-specific mortality, for which previous research attributed the short-run aggregate reduction to diabetes and infectious diseases, but we find no robust long-term cause-specific mortality effects, given the absence of flat pre-trends. To examine behavioral channels, we use \textit{Progresa}'s own experimental evaluation data and find no significant effects on labor supply or household composition; an attempt to analyze income and nutrition using the Mexican Household Expenditure and Income Survey similarly yields no statistically significant results, though with limited power. As a secondary exercise, we revisit the earlier short-run mortality findings of \cite{BarhamRowberry2013JDE} and show that those significant and negative estimates are sensitive to weighting and lag specification: minor and similarly defensible changes to the specification attenuate them by half or more and erode statistical significance. The pattern is consistent with the program's design: \textit{Progresa}'s benefits were primarily conditioned on children's schooling, older adults living in extended households were not direct recipients, and adult health conditionalities required only one annual clinic visit.

\p The paper proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{s:progresa} describes the \textit{Progresa} program. Section~\ref{s:data} presents the data, sample, and empirical strategy. Section~\ref{s:results} reports the main mortality results, the replication and cause-of-death analysis, and the mechanisms evidence. Section~\ref{s:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Mexico's \textit{Progresa} CCT Program}\label{s:progresa}

\p \textit{Progresa} began in small rural communities in 1997, providing monetary transfers to poor families conditional on children attending school and family members visiting health clinics. The program rapidly expanded to cover six million families—one quarter of all Mexican families—with two-thirds of beneficiaries residing in communities with fewer than 2,500 inhabitants reaching a take-up of 97\% in the early years \citep{Fiszbein2009CCTBook, ParkerTodd2017JEL}. Average monthly transfers reached approximately MXN~\$800 (US~\$90 PPP), representing more than a 25\% income increase for many poor households \citep{Gertler2004AER, ParkerTodd2017JEL}. All transfers are paid to the female head of household.

\p The program includes two main grant types, both conditional on the household passing a poverty means test. An educational grant is paid to households with children attending school, scaled by the number and grade of enrolled children—the program's primary target. A food and nutrition transfer is paid additionally at a flat rate to every eligible household, conditioned only on health-clinic attendance. Required visit frequency is age-specific: young children and pregnant or lactating women are seen several times a year, but for adults aged 17 and older, including older adults, the requirement is a single annual check-up. Eligible households without school-age children, including many older-adults-only households, therefore still receive the nutrition transfer, but at a substantially lower amount than households also receiving educational grants.\footnote{The fixed nutrition grant amounts to roughly from a sixth to a tenth depending on the seniority of the children attending school \citep{ParkerTodd2017JEL}.} In 2006, a pension component for adults aged 70 and older was added, fundamentally altering the program's relevance for older adults. We end our analysis in 2006 to focus on the original CCT design.

\p The majority of older adult individuals in rural eligible households live in extended families \citep{BehrmanParker2013Demography}. Before the program, approximately 75\% of older adult individuals lived in households with children under 15 \citep{BehrmanParker2013Demography}. In most cases, older adult individuals are grandparents of children receiving educational grants and thus do not directly control program expenditures, though they may benefit indirectly. Given this structure, any mortality effects on older adults would more plausibly emerge gradually through indirect channels, rather than immediately or at large magnitude.

\p Older adult mortality in Mexico has declined over time and is slightly higher in non-marginalized areas than in marginalized (poor) areas, as can be seen by comparing Figure~\ref{f:variation}, Panel a), which covers highly marginalized municipalities, with Appendix Figure~\ref{af:variation_all}, which covers all municipalities. \cite{BeltranSanchezWong2025CSP} demonstrates using panel data from the Mexican Health and Aging Survey that this pattern is not explained by the selective migration of unhealthy individuals toward urban areas.

\section{Data and Empirical Strategy}\label{s:data}

\subsection{Data}\label{s:sources}

\p \textbf{Mortality data.} We use individual-level death registries derived from death certificates, covering every death in Mexico between 1991 and 2006—over three million records. The data include cause of death, birth year, sex, and the residence of the deceased, which we use to link to population denominators and program variables. All data are publicly available from the Mexican National Institute of Statistics and Geography (INEGI).\footnote{INEGI derives mortality data from the certification system of the Mexican Ministry of Public Health. Mexico generally maintains high-quality vital statistics \citep{mahapatra2007civil, mikkelsen2015global}.}

\p The expansion of Seguro Popular—Mexico's public health insurance program for informal-sector workers, which rolled out staggered across municipalities beginning in 2002—may bias our estimates of Progresa's mortality effects after 2002 to the extent the rollout is correlated with Progresa intensity. To address this, we control for Seguro Popular enrollment intensity.\footnote{Seguro Popular reached full municipal coverage by 2006, but significant portions of the eligible population remained uninsured throughout our study period.}

\p \textbf{\textit{Progresa} administrative data.} We obtain end-of-year total counts of enrolled \textit{Progresa} households from administrative records, which we use as the numerator to construct our treatment variable.

\p \textbf{Population and Household data} We linearly interpolate Census data (1990, 2000, 2005, 2010, 2020) on household and population counts to use as the denominator for the mortality rate at the sex, geography, and age cell and treatment intensity variable, respectively.


\p \textbf{Additional controls.} We use the 1990 CONAPO Marginalization Index to restrict the sample to highly marginalized areas, and to implement robustness checks across socioeconomic strata trends. The Marginalization Index classifies all Mexican municipalities into five categories based on household poverty indicators, including dirt floors, crowding, illiteracy, and lack of sanitation infrastructure.

\p \textbf{\textit{Progresa} experimental survey data.} To examine short-run mechanisms, we use the \textit{Progresa} evaluation surveys—the baseline household survey (ENCASEH, 1997) and follow-up rounds (ENCEL, 1998--2000)—collected in 506 treatment and 320 control localities across seven Mexican states (Guerrero, Hidalgo, Michoacán, Puebla, Querétaro, San Luis Potosí, and Veracruz). Treatment localities began receiving \textit{Progresa} transfers in early 1998; control localities were enrolled in late 1999, providing an 18-month experimental window for comparison. The surveys collected individual-level information on labor supply, household composition, and income for all household members. Our mechanisms sample is restricted to older adults aged 65 and older living in eligible households, identified using the program's eligibility criteria applied to the 1997 baseline. 

\subsection{Sample and Descriptive Statistics}\label{s:sample}

\p We construct municipality-year cells combining death counts with population denominators because the municipality is the finest geographic unit at which death records and population denominators are consistently observed across our 1991--2006 panel. Therefore, we implement a municipality-level measure of treatment intensity to examine mortality among older adults, and given the program targeted the poorer, we restrict our set of municipalities to those highly marginalized using the marginality index.\footnote{The high and very high among the five levels on the 1990 CONAPO Marginalization Index. \cite{Skoufias2001WD} compare the targeting algorithm with consumption- and geography-based alternatives and conclude that the program performed well in targeting the poorest.} Table~\ref{t:descriptives} reports summary statistics comparing marginalized and non-marginalized municipalities in the pre-program period. Panel A presents the cumulative intensity of the treatment variable in the early phase (1997-1999) and the later phase (1997-2005), and Panel B presents the socioeconomic components used to compute the marginality index. As expected, marginalized municipalities have substantially higher \textit{Progresa} enrollment rates and worse socioeconomic conditions. 

\p Figure \ref{f:variation}, Panel a) shows a declining trend in mortality rates after the program started, in line with an increase in the intensity of treatment. The pattern in raw trends suggests that the increase in coverage after the program may be driving the trend observed among females and males. Appendix Figure \ref{af:variation_all} shows a similar trend, but smoother, without a suggestive break at the beginning of the program rolled out.

\subsection{Empirical Strategy}\label{subsec:empirical_strategy}

\p We use the variation in the proportion of households enrolled in \textit{Progresa} across municipalities before and after the program began in 1997 following \cite{parker2023conditional}, who study the long-run effects of \textit{Progresa} among municipalities with the same cumulative enrollment intensity by the end of 2005, focusing on those that saw more intensity in the early phase. Figure \ref{f:variation}, panels b) and c), document geographic spread and temporal dynamics that motivate the two-phase design. The spatial distribution of \textit{Progresa} intensity shows sufficient variation during the early phase (clearer vs darkest areas) that continued to increase by 2005 (mostly darker areas), and thus needs to be addressed using the cumulative enrollment intensity in 2005.\footnote{Appendix Figure ~\ref{af:variation_maps} together with Figure ~\ref{f:variation} shows a similar pattern between highly marginalized and all municipalities intensities, and confirms that mostly highly marginalized municipalities were targeted during the early phase, as shown by the darker areas. Panel c) and d) show that there is not enough variation in 1997 given the program was still rolling out, and the need to focus on the 1997 to 1999 period to capture the initial roll-out.}

\p As mentioned in section \ref{s:progresa}, the program was mean-tested using the marginality index and rolled out at the locality level, but given the lack of locality-level identifiers for death records, we aggregate the treatment at the municipality level. In Figure \ref{af:enroll_loc_mun}, we show that aggregating intensity at the municipality level replicates 
the locality-level patterns for both phases, revealing a more intense roll-out in more marginalized places. The greater overlap between municipalities with similar socio-economic conditions but different roll-out timing is useful for our difference-in-differences design, because municipalities with similar socio-economic conditions may be more likely to follow parallel trends in potential outcomes.\footnote{For further analysis on differences between the locality and municipality level intensity see \cite{parker2023conditional}}

\p Because \textit{Progresa} was preferentially targeted toward more disadvantaged areas, a simple difference-in-differences comparing \textit{Progresa} municipalities to all others could be confounded by differential trends. Restricting the sample to highly marginalized municipalities mitigates this concern. Our identification strategy further refines variation to capture the impact of earlier versus later cumulative exposure to the program (rather than ever-versus-never), under the assumption that mortality effects are cumulative. We estimate:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:did}
\text{MR}_{m,t}
= \beta_{0}\,\textit{Post}_{t}\times\textit{Intensity}_{m,1999}
+ \beta_{1}\,\textit{Post}_{t}\times\textit{Intensity}_{m,2005}
+ \delta_{m} + \gamma_{t} + \chi_{m,t} + \varepsilon_{m,t}
\end{equation}

\p \noindent where $\text{MR}_{m,t}$ is the mortality rate (deaths per 1,000 inhabitants aged 65+) in municipality $m$ in year $t$. $\textit{Post}_{t}$ equals one for years 1997--2006 and zero for 1991--1996. Municipality fixed effects $\delta_{m}$ control for time-invariant unobservables; year fixed effects $\gamma_{t}$ absorb common time trends. $\chi_{m,t}$ is the Seguro Popular enrollment intensity, included in specifications that control for this potentially confounding program. Standard errors are clustered at the municipality level. Throughout this analysis, our coefficient of interest is $\beta_0$, which captures the effect of early-phase enrollment intensity ($\textit{Intensity}_{1999} \times \textit{Post}$); $\beta_1$ controls for later-phase enrollment, as previously described. We retain the level outcome in the main analysis for three reasons: it eases interpretation (effects in deaths per 1,000), it preserves comparability across outcome definitions and with prior work, and it avoids discarding municipality-year cells with zero deaths that a log transformation would require.\footnote{\cite{ChenRoth2024QJE} formalizes the problems that arise when taking logs in the presence of zeros and recommends count-based alternatives. We accordingly address this in the appendix by estimating the same specification using raw death counts (Poisson regression) and log-transformed outcomes—both yield qualitatively similar results to the level specification.}

\p We also estimate event study specifications to trace dynamic effects and test for differential pre-trends:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:eventstudy}
\text{MR}_{m,t}
= \sum_{k \neq 1996} \beta_{k}\,\mathbf{1}[t=k]\times\textit{Intensity}_{m,1999}
+ \sum_{k \neq 1996} \theta_{k}\,\mathbf{1}[t=k]\times\textit{Intensity}_{m,2005}
+ \delta_{m} + \gamma_{t} + \chi_{m,t} + \varepsilon_{m,t}
\end{equation}

\p \noindent with 1996 as the reference year. The $\beta_{k}$ coefficients trace the effect of early-phase enrollment intensity on mortality in each year relative to 1996.

\p As previously mentioned, our interaction design, following \cite{parker2023conditional}, anchors identification to a discrete break in 1997 and explicitly controls for later-phase enrollment, separating the effects of the two rollout phases.\footnote{Both measures are cumulative, so $\textit{Intensity}_{1999}$ and $\textit{Intensity}_{2005}$ are mechanically positively correlated in levels: their correlation in our sample is $0.61$, weighting by the older adult population.} What varies across municipalities is the \textit{path}: because the rollout ramps up toward near-universal coverage, municipalities that enrolled heavily in 1997--1999 have less remaining enrollment to add in 2000--2005, while those that started slowly catch up later.\footnote{Early-phase intensity is therefore negatively related to the later-phase \textit{increment}, and omitting the late-phase control would conflate overall program intensity with the timing of exposure that drives our identification.} Including $\textit{Intensity}_{2005} \times \textit{Post}$ as a control ensures that $\beta_0$ captures the effect of earlier cumulative exposure specifically, holding constant the later enrollment trajectory.\footnote{Recent developments in difference-in-differences estimators with staggered adoption, using continuous time-varying treatment intensity \citep{deChaisemartinDHaultfoeuille2024IntertemporalDiD}, allow us to compare municipalities whose intensity changes from year to year ("switchers") to municipalities whose intensity is flat ("stayers"). In our setting, treatment intensity keeps rising across nearly all municipalities throughout the study period, leaving few stayers to serve as valid controls. In our design, treatment timing is common across all municipalities, so we never compare already-treated to newly-treated units, preventing forbidden comparisons \citep{callaway2021difference, sun2021estimating, de2020two}} Therefore,  $\beta_0$ measures the average effect of a one-unit increase in early-phase enrollment intensity on the mortality rate for adults aged 65 and older, conditional on later-phase intensity, and aligns with the population-level policy question: what was the average effect of \textit{Progresa} on mortality among older Mexican adults living in marginalized areas?\footnote{\textcolor{red}{Because treatment is measured as municipality-level enrollment intensity rather than household-level receipt, $\beta_0$ is an intent-to-treat effect: the reduced-form impact of municipal program exposure on older adult mortality, whether or not a given older adult's own household received transfers. The restriction to highly marginalized municipalities defines the population over which that effect is estimated, namely \textit{Progresa}'s intended target areas.}} 

\p Identification relies on the assumption that, conditional on municipality and year fixed effects and later-phase enrollment intensity, municipalities with higher $\textit{Intensity}_{1999}$ would have followed parallel mortality trends to those with lower intensity in the absence of the program. This assumption would be violated if the timing of early enrollment was systematically related to pre-existing health infrastructure, local state capacity, or other municipality-level factors that independently affected older adult mortality trends. The event study format of equation~(\ref{eq:eventstudy}) allows us to test this assumption directly. We also apply population weights because \textit{Progresa} targeted the most isolated and impoverished communities first; unweighted estimates may overstate the average effect by giving excess weight to the highest-intensity, smallest municipalities, whose mortality rates are also more volatile. Weighting by the older adult population gives each municipality influence proportional to its contribution to total older adult deaths.

\section{Results}\label{s:results}

\subsection{Main Results}\label{s:main_results}

\p Table~\ref{t:did_age} reports difference-in-differences estimates of the effect of \textit{Progresa} enrollment intensity on the mortality rate for adults aged 65 and older. We report our parameter of interest, $\beta_0$, as $\textit{Intensity}_{1999} \times \textit{Post}$, and we did not report the $\beta_1$ ($\textit{Intensity}_{2005} \times \textit{Post}$) because it is not a causally interpretable parameter but rather a control. Across all specifications, the estimated effect of \textit{Progresa} enrollment intensity on older adults' mortality is small and statistically insignificant. The interaction of early-phase intensity is close to zero for the pooled sample and separately by sex. Adding a socioeconomic trend proxied by the marginality index to account for differences at baseline does not materially alter results. These findings hold regardless of whether we stratified by age of the older adult population.\footnote{In Appendix Table \ref{at:age_groups}, we extend the age group analysis without no significant effects for adjacent age groups and different age ranges.}

\p Figure~\ref{f:es_dd_sex_no_weight} presents the event-study estimates for the main specification Table~\ref{t:did_age}, column~1. The pre-program coefficients show variability, particularly among males, raising concerns about the strength of the parallel trends assumption. In the post-program period, coefficients fluctuate around zero and do not have a persistent decline in mortality, consistent with the null average DiD estimate documented in Table~\ref{t:did_age}. For females, while it shows less pre-trends, post-period effects are mostly zero. Appendix Figure \ref{af:ses_trend} shows alternative socioeconomic trends interactions, without altering the findings.

\p \textbf{Cause-of-death analysis} Following previous work suggesting cause of death paths on mortality reduction, we decompose the aggregate null result into nine cause-specific components: cancer, cardiovascular disease, respiratory disease, infectious disease, diabetes, nutritional causes, ill-defined causes, accidents, and other.\footnote{We follow \cite{BarhamRowberry2013JDE}'s cause classification so that the decomposition is comparable to theirs.} Appendix Table~\ref{at:did_robust_cod} and Figures~\ref{af:es_cod_marg_a}--\ref{af:es_cod_marg_b} present DiD estimates and event studies for this exploratory decomposition. Early-phase enrollment ($\textit{Intensity}_{1999} \times \textit{Post}$) yields negative point estimates for cancer, ill-defined causes, and diabetes, alongside a positive point estimate for cardiovascular mortality. Only cancer survives Romano-Wolf step-down correction for the familywise error rate across the nine hypotheses tested jointly (all other adjusted $p$-values exceed $0.05$), so the remaining individually significant estimates likely reflect multiple-testing variation rather than genuine cause-specific impacts.\footnote{\cite{BarhamRowberry2013JDE} deliberately include cancer as one of two causes ``less likely to be affected by Progresa'' and treat their small, insignificant estimate as evidence that trends in unobservables are not biasing their results. There is no plausible mechanism by which a cash transfer with an annual health check-up would reduce cancer mortality over this horizon, so we read our own estimate on this outcome as a sign of residual differential trends in the cause-specific decomposition rather than as a program effect.}
% so our substantially larger and statistically significant estimate on the same placebo outcome is a signal that residual differential trends contaminate the cause-specific decomposition, not that the program reduced cancer deaths.} The significant coefficient on ill-defined causes warrants additional caution, as it may capture changes in cause-of-death coding or registration practices over the panel rather than true mortality reductions.\footnote{We implement \cite{BarhamRowberry2013JDE} classification for comparison.}
The event studies in Appendix Figures~\ref{af:es_cod_marg_a}--\ref{af:es_cod_marg_b} are broadly consistent with this interpretation: post-period confidence intervals overlap substantially with the pre-period distribution for most causes, providing no clear visual evidence of a break at program rollout. We therefore conclude that the cause-specific results are consistent with the aggregate null rather than indicative of heterogeneous program effects that cancel in the aggregate.

\subsection{Robustness}\label{s:robustness}

\p We first assess robustness to functional form and outcome definition. Appendix Table~\ref{at:did_robust_ff} presents DiD estimates under four specifications: column~(1) is the baseline mortality rate in levels; column~(2) uses the natural log of the mortality rate; column~(3) uses Poisson pseudo-maximum likelihood with raw death counts; and column~(4) replaces the mortality rate with the Age-Adjusted Mortality Rate (AAMR), which directly standardizes for the age structure of each municipality's older adults population.  Estimates remain small and statistically insignificant across all columns.\footnote{Appendix Figure~\ref{af:es_func_form} presents the corresponding event study estimate; the pre- and post-period patterns are qualitatively similar to the baseline level specification in all cases.} We then test whether our results are sensitive to the year at which later-phase cumulative enrollment is measured. The 2005 endpoint is not an arbitrary empirical choice: following \cite{parker2023conditional}, it marks the close of the second roll-out phase, so that $\beta_0$ compares municipalities reaching the \textit{same} cumulative enrollment by the end of that phase but arriving there at different speeds. However, Appendix Figure~\ref{af:beta0_stability} shows the year-by-year $\beta_k$ profile is essentially unchanged when we replace it with the adjacent 2004 and 2006 snapshots. Omitting the later-phase control altogether, by contrast, visibly shifts the profile---confirming that what matters is conditioning on the second phase,

\p We next examine the relationship between our long-run findings and the short-run results of \cite{BarhamRowberry2013JDE} (hereafter BR). BR provided the first systematic evidence that \textit{Progresa} reduced older adults mortality, using a design in which mortality is regressed on a two-year lagged enrollment intensity measure without a pre/post interaction:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:br}
\text{MR}_{m,t}
= \alpha_{0} + \alpha_{1}\,\textit{Intensity}_{m,t-2}
+ \delta_{m} + \gamma_{t} + \chi_{m,t} + \varepsilon_{m,t}
\end{equation}

\p Appendix Table~\ref{at:did_robust_br} reproduces their exercise, restricting to municipalities incorporated into \textit{Progresa} in 1998 or 1999. Row~(A) reports their original pooled estimate; row~(B) shows our close replication with a comparable coefficient.\footnote{\textcolor{red}{Our replication uses 1,422 municipalities against the 1,961 BR report. The difference is almost entirely one of which phase-in cohorts are counted, not of data coverage: a further 563 municipalities in our panel first enrolled in 1997, and adding them gives 1,985, within about one percent of BR's figure. Boundary harmonization and our panel-completeness requirement together account for the small remainder.}} This confirms that data and procedural differences are not the source of the divergence between their results and ours. When population weights are added in row~(C), the estimate attenuates by approximately 50\% and loses precision, reflecting the disproportionate influence of small, high-intensity municipalities in the unweighted specification. Rows~(D) and~(E) show that replacing the time-varying denominator with a fixed value before the program start, and also a three-year lag, further reduces the estimated coefficient, underscoring the sensitivity of the finding to this specification choice.\footnote{We also test weather our main specification is robust to the use of a fixed pre-program number of households as denominator on the intensity of treatment, and thus address potential effects the program would have had on household count. We do not find any evidence that would change our conclusions, and we kept the time-varying denominator comparable to that used by \cite{BarhamRowberry2013JDE}, and to maintain internal consistency with the time-varying population denominator in the mortality rate.}

\p BR's specification regresses mortality on a time-varying two-year lag of enrollment intensity, which is defined continuously across all years in the sample. Because there is no fixed pre/post break in their design, it does not yield a set of pre-program coefficients from which to test for differential trends between higher- and lower-intensity municipalities before the program began. Applying our interaction design to their sample and window restructures the same identifying variation into a pre/post framework without altering the sample composition or the underlying cross-sectional intensity variation. This yields three advantages over their original approach. First, a single common break in 1997 removes any variation in treatment \textit{timing} across municipalities, so no already-treated municipality is ever used as a control for one whose enrollment is still rising---the forbidden comparison that a time-varying intensity measure combined with two-way fixed effects generates. Second, it enables the pre-trend test their design lacks. Third, it places the short-run estimates on a directly comparable footing with our long-run design, permitting validation across both samples and time horizons. 

\p Specifically, we replace the time-varying lagged intensity with a fixed early-phase measure interacted with a post-1997 indicator:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:didadapted}
\text{MR}_{m,t}
= \beta_{0}\,\textit{Post}_{t}\times\textit{Intensity}_{m,1999}
+ \delta_{m} + \gamma_{t} + \varepsilon_{m,t}
\end{equation}

\p Appendix Table~\ref{at:did_robust_br2_2002ctrl_eoy} reports estimates under this adapted design for both the BR sample and the full highly marginalized sample over 1992--2002. In the BR sample, the unweighted estimate is negative and statistically significant; adding population weights attenuates it by approximately 50\%—from roughly $-4.0$ to $-1.7$ deaths per 1,000—consistent with the sensitivity to weighting documented in Appendix Table~\ref{at:did_robust_br}. Once we include a later-phase control, such as total cumulative enrollment by the end of the period ($\textit{Post}_{t}\times\textit{Intensity}_{m,2005}$), the results are insignificant.  

\p When the full highly marginalized sample is used, Columns (4) to (6) show the effect is not statistically significant in any specification. The short-run results are therefore sensitive to both sample composition and weighting: the most preferred estimate under the adapted design is approximately $-1.6$ deaths per 1,000, and it does not survive extension to the broader sample. Together, these results suggest that the divergence between BR's short-run finding and our long-run null is at least partly explained by sample and specification choices such as weighting, and that the short-run effect—if real—was more modest and less robust than BR's original estimates imply. Appendix Table~\ref{at:br_trimming} shows that progressively excluding the smallest municipalities (the bottom 10\% and then 25\% of the older-adult population distribution) monotonically shrinks the unweighted estimate. Splitting the sample into population terciles shows the estimate is $-7.343$ in the smallest tercile, and a statistically insignificant $-1.403$ in the largest. The measured effect is thus concentrated in precisely the smallest and most isolated municipalities, which are the least representative of the program's broader target population and whose mortality rates are the most volatile.

\p Appendix Figure~\ref{af:es_sample_compare} presents event study estimates by sample and sex. For the BR phase-in sample, the male panel displays clear differential pre-trends in the pre-program period—a violation sufficient by itself to question the credibility of identification in that subsample. For females in the BR sample, pre-trends are less pronounced, but post-program coefficients do not exhibit a consistent break from the pre-period level, making the aggregate inference inconclusive. In the highly marginalized sample, neither sex produces post-program coefficients distinguishable from pre-period variation. The pooled short-run estimate of approximately $-1.6$ deaths per 1,000 is thus not corroborated by the event study: the visual evidence does not display the clean post-1997 break that would be required to credibly attribute the short-run table estimates to \textit{Progresa}. 

\p To put the magnitudes in perspective, \cite{BarhamRowberry2013JDE}'s original estimate of $-6.37$ translates into approximately one year of gain in life expectancy at age 65: against their sample's pre-program mean and its mean early-phase enrollment intensity of 31.6\%, it implies a reduction of roughly 2 deaths per 1,000 at the sample mean intensity. Appendix Table~\ref{at:did_robust_br2_2002ctrl_eoy}, column (2), is our closest comparable replication; once population weights are added the estimate attenuates to $-1.660$, which translates into roughly 0.5 fewer deaths per 1,000 at mean intensity—a hypothetical gain of at most 0.2--0.3 years of life expectancy using a constant-hazard approximation.\footnote{Using a constant-hazard (exponential) survival approximation: $e_{65} = 1/\mu$ where $\mu$ is the annual mortality rate. Against the Barham-Rowberry sample's pre-program mean of 47.45 deaths per 1,000 ($\mu \approx 0.0475$), baseline life expectancy at 65 is $1/0.0475 \approx 21.1$ years. Evaluated at that sample's mean early-phase intensity of 31.6\%, the $-1.660$ estimate implies a new rate of $\approx 0.0469$, yielding $e_{65} \approx 21.3$ years—a gain of approximately 0.24 years, or 0.76 years at full intensity.} This is around a quarter of what BR report, and is not supported by the event study. Our long-run design finds no statistically significant effect through 2006. We therefore conclude that neither the short-run nor the long-run evidence provides a sufficient basis to claim that \textit{Progresa} produced meaningful reductions in older adults' mortality.\footnote{Our design has enough power to detect meaningful effects. At 80\% power and two-sided 5\% significance (MDE $=2.80\times SE$), the pooled specification (Table~\ref{t:did_age}, column~1; $SE=1.464$) can detect effects as small as 4.10 deaths per 1,000, which clears \cite{BarhamRowberry2013JDE}'s original unweighted estimates of $-6.37$. Our null result is therefore not a power artifact relative to BR's originally reported magnitude.}

\subsection{Mechanisms: Labor Supply, Household Composition, and Income Effects}\label{s:mechanisms}

\p We take additional steps to examine the behavioral channels through which \textit{Progresa} could plausibly have affected older adults health: the annual health check-up conditionality, income and nutrition, and living arrangements.We leverage the experimental design of the original \textit{Progresa} evaluation. In 1997, treatment and control localities were prospectively randomized at the locality level before any program transfers were made. This randomized assignment provides a clean identification strategy that does not require additional controls: any differences in outcomes between treatment and control groups in the post-program years (1998--1999) represent the unconfounded causal effects of early \textit{Progresa} enrollment on older adults' household members' behavior. This design is particularly valuable because older adults individuals were not the intended direct program beneficiaries; they benefit indirectly through household-level income changes or alterations to living arrangements, and only directly through the small food component of the program . The experimental evidence thus provides a test of whether \textit{Progresa} activated the three theoretical pathways that could plausibly benefit older adults.

\p We estimate:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:exp_did}
Y_{ij,t} = \alpha + \beta_t + \gamma \mathds{1}(\text{Treatment})_i + \delta_t \mathds{1}(\text{Treatment})_i + \mu_m + \varepsilon_{ij,t}
\end{equation}

\p where $Y$ is the outcome of interest (weekly hours worked, living alone, etc.), indexed by individual $i$, household $j$, and year $t$. The treatment indicator $\mathds{1}(\text{Treatment})_i$ is randomized at the locality level. $\beta_t$ represents year fixed effects (with 1997 as the omitted baseline), $\gamma$ captures the treatment indicator (with control as the omitted category). The coefficient of interest $\delta_t$ captures the treatment effect in each year t relative to the control group in the baseline year. Municipality fixed effects $\mu_m$ control for time-invariant differences across localities. Standard errors are clustered at the locality level. Because treatment was randomized before outcome realization, the specification requires no additional controls, and non-zero coefficients represent the causal impact of early \textit{Progresa} enrollment on older adults' household members.

\p Table~\ref{t:combined_elderly} reports the results, comparing treatment and control localities within eligible households. Column~(1) reports the effect on weekly hours worked by older adult individuals; columns~(2)--(4) report effects on the probability of living alone, living with children, and living exclusively with other older adult members; and column~(5) reports total health-facility visits.\footnote{Because utilization was not asked at baseline, column~(5) follows \cite{Gertler2000IFPRI} and is estimated as a treatment-control cross-section pooling the two 1999 survey waves rather than as a difference-in-differences.} The estimates show no significant changes: \textit{Progresa} did not reduce older adults' labor hours, did not systematically alter whether older adults lived with children or alone, and did not raise their health-facility visits. The null effects on living arrangements provide little support for the informal-caregiving pathway as an important mechanism in this setting. Columns~(6) and~(7) repeat the labor and health outcomes for older-adults-only households, which receive only the food component of the transfer and therefore isolate the response; we find no evidence that this direct component had a different effect. Appendix Table~\ref{at:expenditures_elderly} extends this analysis to household expenditure outcomes stratified by older adults' household presence; the estimated differential effects for households with older adult members are similarly imprecise and not statistically significant.

\p Complementing the experimental evidence, we examine the income and nutrition channels using the National Household Income and Expenditure Survey (ENIGH), drawing on the mechanisms framework of \cite{Menares2026JHR} for the \textit{70 y Más} pension program. We estimate the effects of \textit{Progresa} intensity on older adults households' labor income, food consumption by category, and out-of-pocket health spending.\footnote{We estimate difference-in-differences models using ENIGH waves from 1992 to 2006, exploiting variation in \textit{Progresa} enrollment intensity across municipalities, following the same two-phase interaction design as in equation~(\ref{eq:did}). Outcome variables include individual labor income, household food expenditure by category, and health spending.} All estimated coefficients are imprecise with large standard errors, implying limited statistical power. These null ENIGH results do not contradict the experimental null from Table~\ref{t:combined_elderly}—they reinforce it, providing convergent evidence from two independent data sources that the annual health check-up, income, nutrition, and caregiving channels through which \textit{Progresa} might have reached older adults members were not activated in ways large enough to reduce mortality.

\section{Conclusion}\label{s:conclusion}

\p This paper examines the long-term effects of Mexico's \textit{Progresa} conditional cash transfer program on older adults mortality. Using sixteen years of vital statistics and administrative enrollment records, we do not find evidence that \textit{Progresa} reduced mortality among adults aged 65 and older during its first ten years of operation. These results are coherent with not finding evidence that a CCT targeting women with children can plausibly reach older adults through three established channels: annual health check-up conditionality, household income and nutrition effects, and changes in co-residence and informal care. Taken together, the picture is of a program that nudges older adults' household members toward the healthcare system once a year but does not produce the sustained changes in income, nutrition, or caregiving needed to reduce mortality over a decade. The annual health check-up conditionality—one visit per year—is too minimal a clinical intervention to drive the mortality reductions.

\p We further show that the key prior findings of \cite{BarhamRowberry2013JDE} are sensitive to specification and sample. Their result attenuates by 50\% with population weighting, and, combined with our design, sample choice, and our own estimates, we conclude that there is no robust evidence of large effects on older adult mortality from Progresa's household-based conditional cash transfer program. Notwithstanding, we assess the credibility of the magnitudes with the broader literature computing the implied elasticity of older adult female mortality with respect to Progresa income.\footnote{We focus on females given that \cite{Menares2026JHR} reported significant effects only for females. Therefore, for the income denominator, we use the estimated Progresa transfer effect (\$50.18/month) and the pre-program baseline income (\$252.14/month) for female-headed households with older adult members, from our household-level ENIGH regressions, yielding a percentage income change of 19.9\%.} For this exercise, we use \cite{BarhamRowberry2013JDE}'s adapted specification to our design, which implies an elasticity of $-0.25$—a value close to the $-0.23$ estimate of elasticity for Mexico's \textit{70 y Más} non-contributory pension.\footnote{We use Appendix \ref{at:did_robust_br2_2002ctrl_eoy}, Panel B, column 2, weighted estimate, in line with the -6.016 unweighted estimate, which is close to their reported -6.46. The -2.3 estimate should not be read as the surviving, comparable version of BR's finding. Instead, it is the largest estimate that remains once weights are applied, not one that withstands the design's own checks. Once we condition on later-phase enrollment and restrict to highly marginalized, all results are insignificant; furthermore, the event studies underlying both samples show no discernible break at 1997.} However, \textit{70 y Más} transferred resources \textit{directly and unconditionally}, reducing older adult women's mortality in rural areas by 5.5\%, so it is questionable that a CCT whose benefits reach older adults mostly indirectly should produce the same elasticity given the absence of evidence on mechanisms, and no mortality reduction that our design can detect.

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This paper examines the long-term effects of Mexico's conditional cash transfer (CCT) program, \textit{Progresa}, on the mortality of adults ages 65 and older. The analysis uses vital statistics combined with administrative records on program enrollment, along with a difference-in-differences design that compares municipalities with different levels of early enrollment but similar eventual coverage. No evidence was found that earlier exposure to CCTs reduced older-adult mortality. The analysis used Progresa's own experimental evaluation data to examine behavioral channels and found no significant effects on older adults' labor supply and limited  evidence of changes in household composition. In conclusion, there is little evidence of significant benefits in terms of mortality among older adults from \textit{Progresa}'s household-based CCT program.

\noindent \\
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} mortality, conditional cash transfers, \textit{Progresa}, Mexico, older adults \\
\noindent\textbf{JEL codes:} I13, I14, I15, I18
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{s:intro}

\p Older adults have become one of the fastest-growing population groups worldwide as fertility has declined across both developed and developing countries \citep{UN2024WPP}.\footnote{Sustained declines in fertility rates shrink the share of younger cohorts entering the population, while life expectancy gains keep older cohorts alive longer.} In Latin America and the Caribbean, the share of the population aged 65 and older was 9.9 percent in 2024 and is projected to nearly double to 18.9 percent by 2050 \citep{UN2024WPP}. A growing share of this population is concentrated in low- and middle-income countries \citep{WHO2024Ageing}. Population aging in Latin America and the Caribbean is unfolding against a backdrop of persistent material deprivation. According to the most recent estimates, one in five older adults in the region lives in multidimensional poverty, facing simultaneous deficits in income, healthcare access, and social support \citep{CEPAL2024SocialPanorama}.\footnote{By comparison, adults aged 65 and older represent approximately 18\% of the population in OECD countries on \citep{OECD2024Society}, yet their income poverty rate—measured as the share living below half the national median income—stands at around 15 percent \citep{OECD2024Society}} Therefore, understanding how income support can improve the welfare of older adults remains paramount.


\p The health effects of income-support programs for older adults have attracted substantial attention, yet the evidence remains mixed. Some studies find important health benefits \citep{ Case2004PerspectivesAging, BarhamRowberry2013JDE, Aguila2015PNAS, MiglinoNavarrete2023AEJP, Menares2026JHR}, others find no effects \citep{LloydSherlockAgrawal2014JDS}, and still others identify unintended adverse consequences linked to obesity and diet change \citep{FernaldGertlerHou2008JN, KronebuschDamon2019EHB}. Most existing evidence focuses on short-term outcomes.

\p To study the longer-term mortality effects of a large-scale transfer program, this paper leverages Mexico's conditional cash transfer (CCT) program launched in 1997 as the Programa de Educacion, Salud y Alimentacion (\textit{Progresa}) and subsequently renamed Oportunidades and then Prospera. \textit{Progresa} has served as a model for CCT programs in more than 60 countries \citep{Fiszbein2009CCTBook, Baird2014JDEffectiveness, ParkerTodd2017JEL}, and a growing literature evaluates its health impacts \citep{Gertler2004AER, LagardeHainesPalmer2009Cochrane, RiveraHernandez2016HSR, ParkerTodd2017JEL}. However, the program's long-term effects---particularly on mortality among aging adults---remain largely unstudied \citep{ParkerTodd2017JEL}. 


\p In contrast to income-support programs such as non-contributory pensions that target older adults, \textit{Progresa} supports families more generally, with transfers paid to the female head of household and widely publicized conditionality features tied to children. Whether a child-focused CCT can generate mortality benefits for older adults raises a natural question: through which channels could a CCT largely designed for women and families with young children affect older adults' health? Several pathways provide plausible theoretical bases to expect \textit{Progresa} to potentially benefit the health of older adult household members indirectly. First, the annual health check-up conditionality requires \textit{all} household members—including older adults—to go to a series of preventive visits,  creating an opportunity for early detection and treatment of conditions such as infectious diseases, diabetes, and hypertension that disproportionately drive mortality in older age \citep{Angel2017Gerontologist, aguila2024conditional}. Second, the cash transfer raises household income by over 25 percent \citep{Gertler2004AER, ParkerTodd2017JEL}, potentially improving nutrition and healthcare access for older adult members who share household resources. Third, the program could induce changes in the older adult labor supply, which could lower health risks (although it could also worsen health through decreased social interaction). Fourth, if the program induces lower labor supply or increased co-residence of other family members, this could increase the availability of informal caregiving within the household, benefiting older adults' health through established social-support pathways \citep{duflo2000child, BehrmanParker2013Demography}. 

\p Research on the effects of \textit{Progresa} on older adults has focused on a narrow set of outcomes and has relatively short follow-up periods. \cite{BehrmanParker2013Demography} documented improved health service use by older adult women, but not men, six years after implementation. \cite{aguila2024conditional} found that \textit{Progresa} improved hypertension diagnosis and treatment among adults aged 50 and older but did not reduce uncontrolled blood pressure as measured by biomarkers, suggesting that CCTs can raise healthcare use without producing clinically meaningful improvements in chronic disease control. Evidence from CCTs in other settings also suggests some healthcare and morbidity benefits. For example, \cite{ContrerasSuarezMaitra2021JPopEcon} found that Colombia's \textit{Familias en Acci\'on} reduced reported illness among never-targeted household members aged 60 and older in the short term and hospitalization in the medium term, with effects concentrated among those in poor health at baseline. In terms of mortality, the key study in the literature has been \cite{BarhamRowberry2013JDE}, who found short-term reductions in mortality among older adults over the first five years of operation of Progresa. However, no study has examined whether such effects persist or accumulate over a longer time horizon. 

% By contrast, evidence on programs that target older adults directly, such as Mexico's subsequent \textit{70 y Más} non-contributory pension reduced older adults women mortality in rural areas \citep{Menares2026JHR} by 5.5\%. 


\p This paper builds on \cite{BarhamRowberry2013JDE} to examine longer-term effects of \textit{Progresa} on older adult mortality, and in the process also replicates that paper. The analysis follows \cite{parker2023conditional} in focusing on highly marginalized municipalities, which the program most heavily targeted and were most likely to have impacts. Difference-in-difference models are estimated exploiting variation in \textit{Progresa} enrollment intensity across municipalities during the program's early phase (1997--1999) to study mortality between 1991 and 2006. \cite{BarhamRowberry2013JDE} and other previous studies relied on time-varying treatment intensity, but recent developments in difference-in-differences methods have shown that this approach can produce forbidden comparisons, in which municipalities whose enrollment has already stabilized are implicitly used as controls for municipalities still increasing their enrollment.\footnote{Recent work by \cite{deChaisemartinDHaultfoeuille2024IntertemporalDiD} develop a continuous-treatment estimator that avoids this problem by comparing municipalities whose intensity changes from year to year (``switchers'') to municipalities whose intensity is flat (``stayers''). In our setting, treatment intensity rises across nearly all highly marginalized municipalities throughout the study period, leaving few stayers to serve as valid controls.} In the design for the present paper, treatment timing is common across all municipalities, so already-treated units are not compared to newly treated ones. As in \citep{parker2023conditional}, the specification controls for later-phase enrollment to avoid comparing municipalities that would have ended with different total enrollment, reflecting characteristics that could themselves be correlated with mortality. Fixing early-phase intensity at a single point per municipality also allows for including interactions with year indicators and test directly for pre-trends.

\p The main finding of this paper is that earlier exposure to Progresa did not produce detectable reductions in older adults’ mortality during the program’s first 10 years, despite evidence from previous research of improvements in some intermediate health outcomes \citep{aguila2024conditional,  BehrmanParker2013Demography}. This result is robust to weighting, to different sets of socioeconomic trends, to controlling for Seguro Popular (a health insurance program for informal sector workers implemented in 2002), and to alternative outcome measures (levels, logs, counts, and age adjustments). As a secondary exercise, the analysis revisited the earlier short-term mortality findings of \cite{BarhamRowberry2013JDE} and found that these estimates are sensitive to weighting and sample choice: relatively small variations in the specification and sample attenuate the estimates and erode statistical significance. To examine plausible behavioral channels, \textit{Progresa}'s own experimental evaluation data were used. No significant effects on health visits, labor supply, or household composition were found. Analyzing income and nutrition using the Mexican Household Expenditure and Income Survey (Encuesta Nacional de Ingresos y Gastos de los Hogares - ENIGH) similarly yielded no statistically significant results, although with limited power. The pattern of results is consistent with \textit{Progresa}'s design: the program was widely known for its conditionality related to children's schooling, so mental accounting models may have led families to focus the cash transfer on benefits for children. This is compounded by the fact that the female heads of household receiving the benefits were often themselves not older adults. Thus, the program's effects on co-resident older adults may well be diluted compared to the larger mortality effects of pension programs \citep{Menares2026JHR}.

\p The next section of this paper describes the \textit{Progresa} program. Section~\ref{s:data} presents the data, sample, and empirical strategy. Section~\ref{s:results} reports the main mortality results, the replication analysis, and the evidence on mechanisms. Section~\ref{s:conclusion} presents the conclusions of the analysis.

\section{Progresa: Mexico's Conditional Cash Transfer Program}\label{s:progresa}

\p \textit{Progresa} began operations in small rural localities in 1997 to provide cash transfers to poor families conditional on children attending school and family members visiting health clinics. The program rapidly expanded to cover 6 million families—one quarter of all Mexican families—with two-thirds of beneficiaries residing in localities with fewer than 2,500 inhabitants, reaching a take-up of 97 percent in the early years \citep{Fiszbein2009CCTBook, ParkerTodd2017JEL}. Average monthly transfers reached approximately MXN\$800 (US\$90 in purchasing power parity), representing more than a 25 percent income increase for many poor households \citep{Gertler2004AER, ParkerTodd2017JEL}. All transfers were paid to the female head of household.

\p The program includes two main grant types, both conditional on the household passing a poverty means test. An educational grant is paid to households with children attending school. In addition, a food and nutrition transfer is paid at a flat rate to every eligible household, conditioned only on attending appointments at health clinics. The required frequency of such appointments is age-specific: young children and pregnant or lactating women are seen several times a year, but only a single annual check-up is required for adults aged 17 and older, including older adults. Eligible households without school-age children, including many households comprised only of older adults, therefore still receive the nutrition transfer, although it is substantially less than that for households also receiving educational grants. In 2006, a pension component for adults aged 70 and older was added, fundamentally altering the program's relevance for older adults. This paper studies impacts only until 2006 in order to focus on the original CCT design \footnote{Our results are unchanged when 2006 is excluded.} 

%, scaled by the number and grade of enrolled children—the program's primary target

\p Most older adults in rural eligible households live in extended families \citep{BehrmanParker2013Demography}. Before Progresa, approximately 75 percent of older adults lived in households with children under 15 \citep{BehrmanParker2013Demography}. In most cases, older adults are grandparents of children receiving educational grants and thus may not directly control program expenditures, although they may benefit indirectly. Given this structure, any mortality effects on older adults would more plausibly emerge gradually through indirect channels. 

\p Older adult mortality in Mexico has declined over time and is slightly higher in non-marginalized areas than in marginalized (poor) areas, as can be seen by comparing panel a of Figure~\ref{f:variation}, which covers highly marginalized municipalities, with Appendix Figure~\ref{af:variation_all}, which covers all municipalities. Using panel data from the Mexican Health and Aging Study, \cite{BeltranSanchezWong2025CSP} demonstrated that this pattern is not explained by the selective migration of unhealthy people toward urban areas.

\section{Data and Empirical Strategy}\label{s:data}

\subsection{Data}\label{s:sources}

\p \textbf{Mortality data.} The analysis uses individual-level death registries derived from death certificates covering every death in Mexico between 1991 and 2006—over 3 million records. The data include cause of death, birth year, sex, and the residence of the deceased, which are used to link to population denominators and program variables. All data are publicly available from the Mexican National Institute of Statistics and Geography (Instituto Nacional de Estadística y Geografía - INEGI).\footnote{INEGI derives mortality data from the certification system of the Mexican Ministry of Public Health. Mexico generally maintains high-quality vital statistics \citep{mahapatra2007civil, mikkelsen2015global}.}

\p \textbf{\textit{Progresa} administrative data.} End-of-year total counts of enrolled \textit{Progresa} households are obtained from administrative records, which are used as the numerator to construct the treatment variable.

\p \textbf{Population and Household data.} Population by sex and five-year age group comes from the census and population-count years 1990, 1995, 2000, and 2005. We interpolate it geometrically between these years. The resulting population aged 65 and older is the denominator of the mortality rates. Household counts, the denominator of treatment intensity, are linearly interpolated between the same census years.

\p \textbf{Additional controls.} Marginalization Index data for 1990 from Mexico's National Population Council of Mexico (Consejo Nacional de Población - CONAPO) are used to restrict the sample to highly marginalized areas, and to implement robustness checks across socioeconomic strata trends. The Marginalization Index classifies all Mexican municipalities into five categories based on household poverty indicators, including dirt floors, crowding, illiteracy, and lack of sanitation  
infrastructure. Enrollment data on \textit{Seguro Popular}, Mexico's public health insurance program for informal-sector workers, are also used to address potential omitted variable confounding in estimates of \textit{Progresa}'s mortality effects.\footnote{The program rolled out staggered across municipalities beginning in May 2002 and reached 84\% municipal coverage by 2006. However, significant proportions of the eligible population remained unenrolled throughout the period studied here.}

\p \textbf{\textit{Progresa} experimental survey data.} To examine short-term mechanisms on healthcare utilization and family arrangements the analysis uses \textit{Progresa} evaluation surveys: the baseline household survey (1997 Encuesta de Características Socioeconómicas y Demográficas de los Hogares) and follow-up rounds (ENCEL, 1998--2000). The survey data were collected in 506 treatment and 320 control localities across seven Mexican states (Guerrero, Hidalgo, Michoacán, Puebla, Querétaro, San Luis Potosí, and Veracruz). Treatment localities began receiving \textit{Progresa} transfers in early 1998, and control localities were enrolled in late 1999, providing an 18-month experimental window for comparison. The surveys collected individual-level information on labor supply, household composition, and income for all household members. The short-term mechanisms sample used for this analysis is restricted to older adults aged 65 and older living in eligible households, identified using Progresa's eligibility criteria applied to the 1997 baseline. 

\textbf{Mexican Household Expenditure and Income Survey (ENIGH).} To examine mechanisms on labor market outcomes, the analysis used the ENIGH, a biennial survey that collects data on household income and expenditure, sociodemographic characteristics of household members, and housing characteristics.

\subsection{Sample and Descriptive Statistics}\label{s:sample}

\p Municipality-year cells are constructed by combining death counts with population denominators, as the municipality is the best geographic unit at which death records and population denominators are consistently observed across the 1991--2006 panel. Therefore, a municipality-level treatment intensity is computed as the ratio of total enrolled households to all households in a given year, defined here as $\textit{Intensity}_{year}$. Table~\ref{t:descriptives} reports summary statistics comparing marginalized and non-marginalized municipalities in the pre-program period. Panel~A presents the cumulative intensity of the treatment variable in the early phase (1997--1999) and the later phase (1997--2005), and panel B presents the socioeconomic components used to compute the Marginality Index.\footnote{Both measures are cumulative, so $\textit{Intensity}_{1999}$ and $\textit{Intensity}_{2005}$ are mechanically positively correlated in levels: their correlation in the sample is $0.61$, weighting by the older adult population.} As expected, marginalized municipalities have substantially higher \textit{Progresa} enrollment rates and worse socioeconomic conditions. 

\p Panel a of Figure~\ref{f:variation} shows a declining trend in mortality rates after the program started, in line with an increase in treatment intensity. The pattern in raw trends suggests that the subsequent increase in coverage may be driving the trend observed among females and males. Appendix Figure~\ref{af:variation_all} shows a similar but smoother trend, without a suggestive break at the beginning of program roll-out.

\subsection{Empirical Strategy}\label{subsec:empirical_strategy}

\p The analysis uses the variation in the proportion of households enrolled in \textit{Progresa} across municipalities before and after the program began in 1997. This follows \cite{parker2023conditional}, who studied the long-term effects of \textit{Progresa} among municipalities with the same cumulative enrollment intensity by the end of 2005, focusing on those that saw more intensity in the early phase.\footnote{Our specification differs from \citet{parker2023conditional} by differentiating effects by year instead of pre-program year.}  What varies across municipalities is the \textit{path}: because the roll-out ramps up toward near-universal coverage, municipalities that enrolled heavily in 1997--1999 have less remaining enrollment to add in 2000--2005, while those that started slowly catch up later.\footnote{Early-phase intensity is therefore negatively related to the later-phase \textit{increment}, and omitting the late-phase control would conflate overall program intensity with the timing of exposure that drives our identification.}


\p Panels b and c of Figure~\ref{f:variation} document the geographic spread and temporal dynamics that motivate the two-phase design. The spatial distribution of \textit{Progresa} intensity shows sufficient variation during the early phase (clearer versus darkest areas) that continued to increase by 2005 (mostly darker areas), and thus needs to be addressed using the cumulative enrollment intensity in 2005.\footnote{Appendix Figure~\ref{af:variation_maps} together with Figure~\ref{f:variation} show a similar pattern between the intensities of highly marginalized municipalities and all municipalities, and they confirm that mostly highly marginalized municipalities were targeted during the early phase, as shown by the darker areas. Panels~c and d of Appendix Figure~\ref{af:variation_maps} show that there is not enough variation in 1997, given the program was still being rolled out, and that there is the need to focus on the 1997--1999 period in order to capture the initial roll-out.} 

\p As mentioned in Section~\ref{s:progresa}, \textit{Progresa} was mean-tested using the Marginality Index and rolled out at the locality level. However, given the lack of locality-level identifiers for death records, the analysis aggregates the treatment at the municipality level. Appendix Figure~\ref{af:enroll_loc_mun} shows that aggregating intensity at the municipality level replicates 
the locality-level patterns for both phases, revealing a more intense roll-out in more marginalized locations. The greater overlap between municipalities with similar socioeconomic conditions but different roll-out timing is helpful for the difference-in-differences design because municipalities with similar socioeconomic conditions may be more likely to follow parallel trends in potential outcomes.\footnote{See \citet{parker2023conditional} for more analysis on differences between locality and municipality-level intensities.}


\p Because \textit{Progresa} was preferentially targeted toward more disadvantaged areas, comparing \textit{Progresa} municipalities to all others could be confounded by differential trends. However, restricting the sample to highly marginalized municipalities prior to the program (1990) mitigates this concern.\footnote{The National Population Council of Mexico \citep{CONAPO1993} classifies all municipalities into five categories ranging from very high marginality to very low marginality using an index constructed from nine census-based indicators capturing educational attainment, housing conditions, access to basic services, low income, and residence in small localities; higher values indicate greater marginalization. \cite{Skoufias2001WD} compare the targeting algorithm with consumption- and geography-based alternatives and conclude that the program performed well in targeting the poorest.} The  specification anchors identification to a discrete break in 1997 and controls for later-phase enrollment, refining the variation to earlier versus later cumulative exposure rather than ever versus never. Assuming mortality effects are cumulative, the following is estimated:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:did}
\text{MR}_{m,t}
= \beta_{0}\,\textit{Post}_{t}\times\textit{Intensity}_{m,1999}
+ \beta_{1}\,\textit{Post}_{t}\times\textit{Intensity}_{m,2005}
+ \delta_{m} + \gamma_{t} + \chi_{m,t} + \varepsilon_{m,t}
\end{equation}

\p \noindent where $\text{MR}_{m,t}$ is the mortality rate (deaths per 1,000 persons aged 65+) in municipality $m$ in year $t$. $\textit{Post}_{t}$ equals one for 1997--2006 and zero for 1991--1996. Municipality fixed effects $\delta_{m}$ control for time-invariant unobservables; year fixed effects $\gamma_{t}$ absorb common time trends. $\chi_{m,t}$ is the Seguro Popular enrollment intensity. Standard errors are clustered at the municipality level. The parameter of interest is $\beta_0$, which measures the average effect of a one-unit increase in early-phase enrollment intensity ($\textit{Intensity}_{1999} \times \textit{Post}$) on the mortality rate for adults aged 65 and older, conditional on later-phase intensity ($\textit{Intensity}_{2005} \times \textit{Post}$).\footnote{Recent developments in difference-in-differences estimators with staggered adoption, using continuous time-varying treatment intensity \citep{deChaisemartinDHaultfoeuille2024IntertemporalDiD} allow us to compare municipalities whose intensity changes from year to year (``switchers'') to municipalities whose intensity is flat (``stayers''). In our setting, treatment intensity keeps rising across nearly all municipalities throughout the study period, leaving few stayers to serve as valid controls. In our design, treatment timing is common across all municipalities, so we do not compare already-treated to newly treated units, preventing forbidden comparisons \citep{callaway2021difference, sun2021estimating, de2020two}.} Thus, $\beta_0$ captures the effect of earlier program exposure in municipalities with comparable eventual program coverage among older Mexican adults living in highly marginalized areas.\footnote{Because treatment is measured as municipality-level enrollment intensity rather than household-level receipt, $\beta_0$ is an intent-to-treat effect.} The level outcome is retained in the main analysis for three reasons: it eases interpretation (effects in deaths per 1,000 persons); it preserves comparability across outcome definitions and with prior work; and it avoids discarding municipality-year cells with zero deaths that a log transformation would require.\footnote{\cite{ChenRoth2024QJE} formalize the problems that arise when taking logs in the presence of zeros and recommend count-based alternatives. This is addressed in the Appendix by estimating the same specification using raw death counts (Poisson regression) and log-transformed outcomes. Both yield qualitatively similar results to the level specification.}

\p Event-study specifications are also estimated in order to trace dynamic effects and test for differential pre-trends:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:eventstudy}
\text{MR}_{m,t}
= \sum_{k \neq 1996} \beta_{k}\,\mathbf{1}[t=k]\times\textit{intensity}_{m,1999}
+ \sum_{k \neq 1996} \theta_{k}\,\mathbf{1}[t=k]\times\textit{Intensity}_{m,2005}
+ \delta_{m} + \gamma_{t} + \chi_{m,t} + \varepsilon_{m,t}
\end{equation}

\p \noindent with 1996 as the reference year. The $\beta_{k}$ coefficients trace the effect of early-phase enrollment intensity on mortality in each year relative to 1996.

\p Identification relies on the assumption that, conditional on municipality and year fixed effects and later-phase enrollment intensity, municipalities with higher $\textit{Intensity}_{1999}$ would have followed mortality trends parallel to those with lower intensity in the absence of the program. This would be violated if the timing of early enrollment were systematically related to preexisting health infrastructure, local state capacity, or other municipality-level factors independently affecting older adult mortality trends. The event-study format of equation~(\ref{eq:eventstudy}) allows for providing evidence on this assumption. In addition, population weights are applied because \textit{Progresa} targeted the most isolated and impoverished communities first; unweighted estimates may overstate the average effect by giving excess weight to the highest-intensity, smallest municipalities, whose mortality rates are also more volatile. Weighting by the older adult population gives each municipality influence proportional to its contribution to total older adult deaths.
The empirical strategy thus estimates the effect of earlier exposure to \textit{Progresa} by comparing municipalities with different levels of enrollment during 1997–1999 but similar cumulative coverage by 2005. Among highly marginalized municipalities, average enrollment increased from 44 percent of households in 1999 to 72 percent in 2005. Enrollment patterns suggest that households entering \textit{Progresa} during the initial expansion received approximately four to five additional years of exposure relative to those entering during the subsequent expansion. The estimates therefore capture the effects of these additional years of exposure rather than the effects of participation relative to the complete absence of the program. They do not rule out mortality benefits common to earlier- and later-enrolling municipalities or benefits that require longer exposure to emerge.

\section{Results}\label{s:results}

\subsection{Main Results}\label{s:main_results}

\p Table~\ref{t:did_age} reports difference-in-differences estimates of the effect of early-phase ($\textit{Intensity}_{1999} \times \textit{Post}$) of \textit{Progresa} enrollment intensity on the mortality rate for adults aged 65 and older.\footnote{We do not report $\beta_1$ ($\textit{Intensity}_{2005} \times \textit{Post}$) because it is not a causally interpretable parameter but rather a control.} Across all specifications, the effect is small and statistically insignificant, both for the pooled sample and separately by sex. Adding a socioeconomic trend (proxied by the Marginality Index) to account for baseline differences does not materially alter the results.\footnote{Appendix Table~\ref{at:age_groups} extends the analysis to adjacent age groups and alternative age ranges, with similar findings.}

\p Figure~\ref{f:es_dd_sex_no_weight} presents the event-study estimates for the main specification in column (1) of Table~\ref{t:did_age}. The pooled pre-program coefficients in panel~A of the table alternate in sign rather than drifting in one direction, so they indicate year-to-year noise rather than a violation of parallel trends. In the post-program period, coefficients likewise fluctuate around zero and show no persistent decline in mortality, consistent with the null estimate documented in Table~\ref{t:did_age}. Among females, panel~B displays no systematic differential trend between higher- and lower-intensity municipalities, and point estimates are mostly positive and similar over the full 1991--2006 window. For males, panel~C exhibits potential pre-trends and thus no credible effect after the program started. Appendix Figure~\ref{af:ses_trend} shows alternative socioeconomic trend interactions, without altering the findings.\footnote{Appendix Table~\ref{at:no_control_sex} present results using no later-phase control. The pooled results are the only significant ones, but at the 10\%, consistent with the null findings in our main specification.}

\subsection{Robustness}\label{s:robustness}

\p The analysis assessed robustness to functional form and the definition of outcomes. Appendix Table~\ref{at:did_robust_ff} presents difference-in-differences estimates under four specifications: column~(1) uses the baseline mortality rate in levels; column~(2) uses the natural log of the mortality rate; column~(3) uses Poisson pseudo-maximum likelihood with raw death counts; and column~(4) replaces the mortality rate with the age-adjusted mortality rate, which directly standardizes for the age structure of each municipality's older adult population. Estimates remain small and statistically insignificant across all columns.\footnote{Appendix Figure~\ref{af:es_func_form} presents the corresponding event-study estimates. The pre- and post-period patterns are qualitatively similar to the baseline-level specification in all cases. Panel~b shows that the unweighted event study is considerably noisier, as expected when the smallest and most volatile municipalities receive full weight, but its shape and sign track the weighted profile, and it shows no post-program decline.} The results were then tested to determine if they are sensitive to the year at which later-phase cumulative enrollment is measured. The 2005 endpoint is not an arbitrary empirical choice: following \cite{parker2023conditional}, it marks the close of the second roll-out phase, so that $\beta_0$ compares municipalities reaching the \textit{same} cumulative enrollment by the end of that phase but arriving there at different speeds. However, Appendix Figure~\ref{af:beta0_stability} shows that the year-by-year $\beta_k$ profile is essentially unchanged when it is replaced with the adjacent 2004 and 2006 snapshots.

\p Given that the results do not suggest that Progresa has reduced old-age mortality, an attempt is made to reconcile these results with the short-term results of. Those authors provided the first systematic evidence that \textit{Progresa} reduced older adults' mortality, using a design in which mortality is regressed on a two-year lagged enrollment intensity measure:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:br}
\text{MR}_{m,t}
= \alpha_{0} + \alpha_{1}\,\textit{Intensity}_{m,t-2}
+ \delta_{m} + \gamma_{t} + \chi_{m,t} + \varepsilon_{m,t}
\end{equation}

\p Appendix Table~\ref{at:did_robust_br} reproduces the exercise of \citet{BarhamRowberry2013JDE} as best as possible by following their article.\footnote{Replication databases were not yet published for \textit{Journal of Development Economics} articles in 2013 when the Barham and Rowberry study was published.} Panel~A reports their original pooled estimate, while panel~B shows our close replication with a comparable coefficient. When population weights are added in panel~C, the estimate attenuates by approximately 50 percent, likely reflecting the disproportionate influence of small, high-intensity municipalities in the unweighted specification. Panels~D and E replace the two-year lag with a three-year and one-year lag, which continue to show lower effects.\footnote{We also test whether our main specification is robust to the use of a fixed pre-program number of households as the denominator for the intensity of treatment, and thus address potential effects the program would have had on household counts. No evidence was found that would change our conclusions, and we maintain the time-varying denominator comparable to that used by \cite{BarhamRowberry2013JDE}, and in order to maintain internal consistency with the time-varying population denominator in the mortality rate.}
%\footnote{We apply BR's own sample rule---a municipality is dated by the year its first locality was phased in---and obtain 1,422 municipalities against the 1,961 they report, from balanced panels of similar size. The gap is not a matter of panel coverage or boundary harmonization; actually, widening the window to 1997--1999 reaches 1,985, and re-estimating the replication on that wider set leaves the coefficient similar, so this discrepancy does not drive the comparison.}
\p The \citet{BarhamRowberry2013JDE} specification regresses mortality on a time-varying two-year lag of enrollment intensity, which is defined continuously across all years in the sample. Because there is no fixed pre-/post-program break in their design, it does not yield a set of pre-program coefficients from which to test for differential trends between higher- and lower-intensity municipalities before the program began.\footnote{\citet{BarhamRowberry2013JDE} tested pre-trends by showing that pre-program (1992–1997) mortality levels were similar year by year between municipalities phased into \textit{Progresa} in 1998 versus 1999 — their preferred comparison sample. As a further check, they also report that lagging the dependent variable, three years yields a small and insignificant effect.} Applying our interaction design to their sample and window restructures the same identifying variation into a pre-/post-program framework without altering the sample composition or the underlying cross-sectional intensity variation. This yields three advantages over their original approach. First, a single common break in 1997 removes any variation in treatment \textit{timing} across municipalities, so no already-treated municipality is used as a control for one whose enrollment is still rising---the forbidden comparison generated by a time-varying intensity measure combined with two-way fixed effects. Second, it enables a strong pre-trend test. Third, it allows for comparison between the short-term estimates with our long-term design, enabling validation across both samples and time horizons.

\p Specifically, we replace the time-varying lagged intensity with a fixed early-phase measure interacted with a post-1997 indicator:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:didadapted}
\text{MR}_{m,t}
= \beta_{0}\,\textit{Post}_{t}\times\textit{Intensity}_{m,1999}
+ \delta_{m} + \gamma_{t} + \varepsilon_{m,t}
\end{equation}

\p Appendix Table~\ref{at:did_robust_br2_2002ctrl_eoy} reports estimates under this adapted design for both the \citet{BarhamRowberry2013JDE} sample and the full highly marginalized sample over 1992--2002. In the  sample, the unweighted estimate is negative and statistically significant. Adding population weights attenuates that estimate by approximately 50 percent—from roughly $-4.0$ to $-1.7$ deaths per 1,000 persons—consistent with the sensitivity to weighting documented in Appendix Table~\ref{at:did_robust_br}. 
%Once we include a later-phase control, such as total cumulative enrollment by the end of the period ($\textit{Post}_{t}\times\textit{Intensity}_{m,2005}$), the results are insignificant. 

\p When the full highly marginalized sample is used, columns (3) and (4) in Appendix Table Table~\ref{at:did_robust_br2_2002ctrl_eoy} show that the effect is not statistically significant in any specification. The short-term results are sensitive to both sample composition and weighting: the closest-weighted estimate under the adapted design is approximately $-1.660$ deaths per 1,000 persons, and it does not survive extension to the relevant sample. Overall, our replication exercises of \citet{BarhamRowberry2013JDE} suggest that their estimates become smaller, depend on sample and specification choices, and are not supported by convincing event-study patterns. 
%Additional results (not shown) indicate that progressively excluding the smallest municipalities (the bottom 10\% and then 25\% of the older-adult population distribution) monotonically shrinks the unweighted estimate. Splitting the sample into population terciles shows the estimate is $-7.343$ in the smallest tercile, and a statistically insignificant $-1.403$ in the largest. The measured effect is thus concentrated in precisely the smallest and most isolated municipalities, which are the least representative of the program's broader target population and whose mortality rates are the most volatile.

\p Appendix Figure~\ref{af:es_sample_compare} presents event-study estimates by sample and sex over the shorter 1992--2002 window. Unweighted and weighted results are presented. For females in the \citet{BarhamRowberry2013JDE} sample, pre-trends are less pronounced, but post-program coefficients do not exhibit a consistent break from the pre-period level, and, if anything, overlap with pre-period effects. The male panels, among weighted results, display clear differential pre-trends in both the \citet{BarhamRowberry2013JDE} phase-in and the highly marginalized samples over this window. In addition, in the highly marginalized sample, neither sex produces post-program coefficients distinguishable from pre-period variation. The pooled short-term estimate of $-1.660$ deaths per 1,000 persons is thus not corroborated by the event study: the visual evidence does not display the clean post-1997 break that would be required to credibly attribute the short-term table estimates to \textit{Progresa}.

\p To put the magnitudes in perspective, the original estimate of \citet{BarhamRowberry2013JDE} of $-6.37$ translates into approximately one year of gain in life expectancy at age 65: against their sample's pre-program mean and its mean early-phase enrollment intensity of 31.6 percent, this implies a reduction of roughly 2 deaths per 1,000 persons at the sample mean intensity. Column (2) of Appendix Table~\ref{at:did_robust_br2_2002ctrl_eoy} is our closest comparable replication; once population weights are added the estimate attenuates to $-1.660$, which translates into roughly 0.5 fewer deaths per 1,000 persons at mean intensity—a hypothetical gain of at most 0.2--0.3 years of life expectancy using a constant hazard approximation.\footnote{Using a constant-hazard (exponential) survival approximation: $e_{65} = 1/\mu$ where $\mu$ is the annual mortality rate. Against the pre-program mean of 47.45 deaths per 1,000 persons ($\mu \approx 0.0475$) in the Barham-Rowberry sample, baseline life expectancy at 65 is $1/0.0475 \approx 21.1$ years. Evaluated at that sample's mean early-phase intensity of 31.6 percent, the $-1.660$ estimate implies a new rate of $\approx 0.0469$, yielding $e_{65} \approx 21.3$ years—a gain of approximately 0.24 years, or 0.76 years at full intensity.} This is around a quarter of what \citet{BarhamRowberry2013JDE} report, and is not particularly supported by the event study. Our long-term design finds no statistically significant effect through 2006. It is therefore concluded here that there is little evidence that \textit{Progresa} has produced meaningful and sustained reductions in older adults' mortality.\footnote{Our design has sufficient power to detect meaningful effects. At 80 percent power and two-sided 5 percent significance, the minimum detectable effect (MDE $=2.80\times SE$), the pooled specification (Table~\ref{t:did_age}, column~(1); $SE=1.464$) can detect effects as small as 4.10 deaths per 1,000 persons, which clears the original unweighted estimates of \cite{BarhamRowberry2013JDE} of $-6.37$. Our null is therefore informative against the originally reported magnitude of rather than a power artifact.}

\subsection{Mechanisms: Labor Supply, Household Composition, and Income Effects}\label{s:mechanisms}

\p This section presents the additional steps taken to examine the behavioral channels through which \textit{Progresa} could plausibly have affected older adults' health: the annual health check-up conditionality, income and nutrition, and living arrangements. The analysis leverages the experimental design of the original \textit{Progresa} evaluation. In 1997, treatment and control localities were prospectively randomized at the locality level before any program transfers were made. This randomized assignment provides a clean identification strategy that does not require additional controls: any differences in outcomes between treatment and control groups in the post-program years (1998--1999) represent the unconfounded causal effects of early \textit{Progresa} enrollment on the behavior of older adults' household members. This design is particularly valuable because older adults were not the intended direct program beneficiaries: they could benefit indirectly through household-level income changes or alterations to living arrangements, and only directly through the small food component of the program. The experimental evidence thus provides a test of whether \textit{Progresa} activated the three theoretical pathways that could plausibly benefit older adults, estimated as follows:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:exp_did}
Y_{ij,t} = \alpha + \beta_t + \gamma \mathds{1}(\text{Treatment})_i + \delta_t \mathds{1}(\text{Treatment})_i + \mu_m + \varepsilon_{ij,t}
\end{equation}

\p where $Y$ is the outcome of interest (weekly hours worked, living alone, etc.), indexed by individual $i$, household $j$, and year $t$. The treatment indicator $\mathds{1}(\text{Treatment})_i$ is randomized at the locality level. $\beta_t$ represents year fixed effects (with 1997 as the omitted baseline), $\gamma$ captures the treatment indicator (with control as the omitted category). The coefficient of interest $\delta_t$ captures the treatment effect in each year t relative to the control group in the baseline year. Municipality fixed effects $\mu_m$ control for time-invariant differences across municipalities. Standard errors are clustered at the locality level. Because treatment was randomized before outcome realization, the specification requires no additional controls, and non-zero coefficients represent the causal impact of early \textit{Progresa} enrollment on older adults' household members.

\p Table~\ref{t:combined_elderly} reports the results, comparing treatment and control localities within eligible households. Column~(1) reports the effect on weekly hours worked by older adult individuals; columns~(2)--(4) report effects on the probability of living alone, living with children, and living exclusively with other older adult members; and column~(5) reports total health facility visits.\footnote{Because use was not asked about at baseline, column~(5) follows \cite{Gertler2000IFPRI} and is estimated as a treatment-control cross-section pooling the two 1999 survey waves rather than as a difference-in-differences.} The estimates show no significant reductions in older adults’ labor hours. Limited evidence is found of changes in living arrangements, although older men were more likely to reside with children in 1999. No statistically significant increase is found in health facility visits measured over the previous four weeks. This result does not necessarily contradict earlier findings of increased healthcare use among older women or improvements in hypertension diagnosis and treatment, which examine different outcomes, populations, and follow-up periods. In particular, a four-week recall period may be insufficient to capture compliance with an annual preventive checkup. Columns~(6) and~(7) repeat the labor and health outcomes for older-adults-only households, which receive only the food component of the transfer and therefore isolate the response. No evidence is found that this direct component had a different effect. Appendix Table~\ref{at:expenditures_elderly} extends this analysis to household expenditure outcomes stratified by older adults' household presence. The estimated differential effects for households with older adult members are similarly imprecise and not statistically significant.

\p Complementing the experimental evidence, the income and nutrition channels are examined using the ENIGH, drawing on the mechanisms framework of \cite{Menares2026JHR} for the \textit{70 y Más} pension program. The effects of \textit{Progresa} intensity are measured on labor income in households with older adults, food consumption by category, and out-of-pocket health spending.\footnote{Difference-in-differences models using ENIGH waves from 1992 to 2006 are used to exploit variation in \textit{Progresa} enrollment intensity across municipalities, following the same two-phase interaction design as in equation~(\ref{eq:did}). Outcome variables include individual labor income, household food expenditure by category, and health spending.} All estimated coefficients are imprecise with large standard errors, implying limited statistical power. These null ENIGH results do not contradict the experimental null from Table~\ref{t:combined_elderly}, although one must be cautious given the statistical inability to reject modest-sized effects. It is notable, however, that these data from a second independent source show limited evidence of changes in the income, nutrition, healthcare, and caregiving mechanisms examined, although imprecision in several estimates prevents ruling out smaller effects.\footnote{Results not shown but available upon request to the authors.}

\section{Conclusion}\label{s:conclusion}

\p This paper has examined the long-term effects of \textit{Progresa}, Mexico's conditional cash transfer program, on the mortality of older adults. Using 16 years of vital statistics and administrative enrollment records, little evidence is found that earlier exposure to \textit{Progresa} reduced mortality among adults aged 65 and older during Progresa's first 10 years of operation. Because the design compares municipalities with similar eventual program coverage, estimates capture the effects of earlier exposure rather than the effects of program participation relative to no participation. The findings do not mean that \textit{Progresa} had no benefits for older adults. Previous research documents improvements in healthcare use and in the diagnosis and treatment of conditions such as hypertension \citep{BehrmanParker2013Demography, aguila2024conditional}. Rather, the results indicate that these improvements did not translate into detectable reductions in mortality over the program’s first decade. Greater contact with the healthcare system or improved detection of chronic disease may be insufficient to reduce mortality without sustained treatment, effective disease management, or additional resources directed specifically toward older adults. These findings underscore the distinction between improvements in intermediate health outcomes and effects on survival. They also point to the importance of evaluating whether changes in healthcare access ultimately produce meaningful long-term health benefits.

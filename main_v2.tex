\begin{abstract}
\noindent
\textcolor{red}{We study the medium- and long-run effects of Mexico's \textit{Progresa} conditional cash transfer (CCT) program on adult mortality at ages 65 and older. Using 15 years of vital statistics combined with administrative records on program enrollment, we evaluate whether almost a decade of exposure to a large-scale CCT translated into detectable reductions in elderly mortality. Using a difference-in-differences design that exploits geographic variation in program intensity across highly marginalized municipalities, we find no evidence of medium- or long-run effects on the elder mortality rate (EMR) for adults aged 65. Our event study reveals that some year-specific coefficients in the early post-program period are individually negative and significant—consistent with the short-run gains reported by prior work for infectious disease and diabetes—but these effects do not persist over the decade and the average DiD estimate is not statistically significant. Earlier short-run estimates that suggested large mortality gains turn out to be sensitive to weighting and lag structure, becoming smaller and statistically insignificant under equally defensible specifications. We argue these findings are coherent with program design: \textit{Progresa}'s transfers were conditioned on children's schooling and health visits and reached the elderly only indirectly through within-household resource sharing, while the adult health conditionality required only a single annual clinic visit. The contrast with non-contributory pensions that target older adults directly underscores how program design—not transfer size alone—shapes mortality outcomes for the elderly.}

\noindent \\
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Mortality, Conditional Cash Transfers, \textit{Progresa}, Mexico, Elderly \\
\noindent\textbf{JEL Codes:} I13, I14, I15, I18
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{s:intro}

\p The health effects of income-support programs for older adults have attracted substantial attention, yet the evidence remains mixed. Some studies find important health benefits \citep{ Case2004PerspectivesAging,Aguila2015PNAS, BarhamRowberry2013JDE}, others find no effects \citep{LloydSherlockAgrawal2014JDS}, and still others identify unintended adverse consequences linked to obesity and diet change \citep{FernaldGertlerHou2008JN, KronebuschDamon2019EHB}. Most existing evidence focuses on short-run outcomes, leaving long-run mortality effects poorly understood.

\p Mexico's \textit{Progresa} conditional cash transfer (CCT) program, launched in 1997 and subsequently renamed Oportunidades and then Prospera, offers the opportunity to study longer-term mortality effects of a large-scale transfer program. \textit{Progresa} has served as a model for CCT programs in over 60 countries \citep{Baird2014JDEffectiveness, Fiszbein2009CCTBook, ParkerTodd2017JEL}, and a growing literature evaluates its health impacts \citep{Gertler2004AER, LagardeHainesPalmer2009Cochrane, ParkerTodd2017JEL, RiveraHernandez2016HSR}. However, long-term effects—particularly on elderly mortality—remain largely unstudied \citep{ParkerTodd2017JEL}.

\p Despite extensive evaluation of \textit{Progresa}'s effects on children, the literature on its consequences for elderly household members is thin and almost entirely short-run. \cite{BehrmanParker2013Demography} document improved health-service utilization for elderly women but not men six years after implementation. \cite{aguila2024conditional} find that \textit{Progresa} improved hypertension diagnosis and treatment use among adults aged 50 and older but did not reduce uncontrolled blood pressure as measured by biomarkers, suggesting that CCTs can raise healthcare utilization without producing clinically meaningful improvements in chronic disease control. \cite{BarhamRowberry2013JDE} reported short-run reductions in aggregate elderly mortality over the first two years of program operation, but no study has examined whether such effects persist or accumulate over a longer horizon. By contrast, evidence on programs that target older adults directly is more developed: Mexico's subsequent \textit{70 y Más} non-contributory pension reduced elderly women mortality in rural areas \citep{Menares2026JHR} by 5.5\%, and \cite{MiglinoNavarrete2023AEJP} find that Chile's non-contributory basic pension reduced four-year mortality by 2.7 percentage points among elderly recipients. Whether a CCT structured around children's conditionalities can deliver comparable mortality benefits to the elderly over a meaningful horizon is the question we take up.

\textcolor{red}{\p These findings raise a natural question: through what channels could a CCT designed for women with young children plausibly affect elderly health? Three pathways are grounded in the program's design. First, the health conditionality requires \textit{all} household members—including the elderly—to attend one annual clinic visit, creating an opportunity for early detection and treatment of conditions such as infectious disease, diabetes, and hypertension that disproportionately drive mortality in older age \citep{Aguila2024EHB, Angel2017Gerontologist}. Second, the cash transfer raises household income by over 25\% \citep{ParkerTodd2017JEL, Gertler2004AER}, potentially improving nutrition and healthcare access for elderly members who share household resources. Third, if program-induced changes in labor or living arrangements alter the availability of informal caregiving within the household, this could affect elderly health through established social-support pathways \citep{BehrmanParker2013Demography}. These channels provide a credible theoretical case for expecting \textit{Progresa} to benefit elderly household members indirectly—even though the program never targeted them directly.}

\p In this paper, we estimate difference-in-differences models exploiting variation in the proportion of households enrolled in \textit{Progresa} across municipalities, comparing municipalities that enrolled more intensively in the program's early phase (1997--1999) to those that enrolled more intensively in the later phase (2000--2005), following the strategy of \cite{parker2023conditional}. We focus on highly marginalized municipalities where the program was most heavily targeted, and study the period 1991--2006, stopping before a major structural change in 2006 when \textit{Progresa} added a direct pension component for the elderly.

\textcolor{red}{\p Our main finding is that \textit{Progresa} had no significant effect on elderly mortality over its first ten years of operation. This result is robust to weighting, inclusion of Seguro Popular controls, and alternative outcome measures (levels, logs, and Poisson). We extend the analysis to cause-specific mortality and find no significant effects for any cause of death—including infectious disease and diabetes, the same causes to which \cite{BarhamRowberry2013JDE} attributed their short-run aggregate reduction—suggesting that even the putative pathways through which a short-run effect could operate did not produce lasting mortality reductions. To examine behavioral channels, we use \textit{Progresa}'s own experimental evaluation data and find no significant effects on elderly labor supply or household composition; an analysis of income and nutrition using the ENIGH survey similarly yields no significant results, though with limited power. As a secondary exercise, we revisit the earlier short-run evidence and show that the positive estimates reported for the first two program years are sensitive to weighting and lag structure: minor and equally defensible changes to the specification attenuate them by half or more and erode statistical significance. We argue the medium- and long-run nulls are consistent with program design: \textit{Progresa}'s benefits were primarily conditioned on children's schooling, elderly individuals living in extended households were not the direct transfer recipients, and health conditionalities required only one annual clinic visit for adults.}

\p The paper proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{s:progresa} describes the \textit{Progresa} program. Section~\ref{s:data} presents the data, sample, and empirical strategy. Section~\ref{s:results} reports the main mortality results, the replication and cause-of-death analysis, and the mechanisms evidence. Section~\ref{s:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Mexico's \textit{Progresa} CCT Program}\label{s:progresa}

\textcolor{red}{\p \textit{Progresa} began in small rural communities in 1997, providing monetary transfers to poor families conditional on children attending school and family members visiting health clinics. The program rapidly expanded to cover six million families—one quarter of all Mexican families—with two thirds of beneficiaries residing in communities with fewer than 2,500 inhabitants \citep{ParkerTodd2017JEL, Fiszbein2009CCTBook}. Average monthly transfers reached approximately MXN~\$800 (US~\$90 PPP), representing more than a 25\% income increase for many poor households \citep{Gertler2004AER, ParkerTodd2017JEL}. All transfers are paid to the female head of household.}

\p The program includes two main grant types: grants conditional on children's school attendance (for children under 22), and a health and nutrition transfer requiring all family members to attend regular health clinic visits. For adults aged 17 and older, including the elderly, the attendance requirement is one clinic visit per year—a minimal conditionality. In 2006, a pension component for adults aged 70 and older was added, fundamentally altering the program's reach for the elderly. We end our analysis in 2006 to focus on the original CCT design.

\textcolor{red}{\p The majority of elderly individuals in rural eligible households live in extended families \citep{BehrmanParker2013Demography}. Before the program, approximately 75\% of elderly individuals lived in households with children under 15 \citep{BehrmanParker2013Demography}. In most cases, elderly individuals are grandparents of children receiving educational grants and thus do not directly control program expenditures, though they may benefit indirectly. Given this structure, large and immediate effects on elderly health and mortality would not be expected.}

\p Elderly mortality in Mexico has declined over time and is slightly higher in non-marginalized areas than in marginalized (poor) areas, as shown in Figure~\ref{f:variation}. \cite{BeltranSanchezWong2025CSP} demonstrate using panel data from the Mexican Health and Aging Survey that this pattern is not explained by selective migration of unhealthy individuals toward urban areas.

\section{Data and Empirical Strategy}\label{s:data}

\subsection{Data}\label{s:sources}

\p \textbf{Mortality data.} We use individual-level death registries derived from death certificates, covering every death in Mexico between 1991 and 2006—over three million records. The data include cause of death, birth year, sex, and the municipality of residence of the deceased, which we use to link to population denominators and program variables. All data are publicly available from the Mexican National Institute of Statistics and Geography (INEGI).\footnote{INEGI derives mortality data from the certification system of the Mexican Ministry of Public Health. Mexico generally maintains high-quality vital statistics \citep{mahapatra2007civil, mikkelsen2015global}.}

\p The expansion of Seguro Popular—Mexico's public health insurance program for informal-sector workers, which rolled out staggered across municipalities beginning in 2002—may have improved the accuracy of death registration. To address this, we control for Seguro Popular enrollment intensity at the municipality level.\footnote{Seguro Popular reached full municipal coverage by 2006 but significant portions of the eligible population remained uninsured throughout our study period.}

\p \textbf{Population data.} We use INEGI population counts disaggregated by sex, municipality, and age from Census data (1990, 2000, 2005, 2010, 2020), linearly interpolating annual estimates for each sex-age-municipality cell. We similarly interpolate municipal household counts to construct program intensity denominators.

\p \textbf{\textit{Progresa} administrative data.} We obtain annual counts of enrolled \textit{Progresa} households by municipality from administrative records, which we use to construct our treatment intensity variable.

\p \textbf{Additional controls.} We use the 1990 CONAPO Municipal Marginalization Index to restrict the sample to highly marginalized areas and as a covariate. We also incorporate administrative data on Seguro Popular beneficiaries to construct a municipality-year enrollment intensity variable.\footnote{The Marginalization Index classifies all Mexican municipalities into five categories based on household poverty indicators, including dirt floors, crowding, illiteracy, and lack of sanitation infrastructure.}

\p \textbf{\textit{Progresa} experimental survey data.} To examine short-run mechanisms, we use the \textit{Progresa} evaluation surveys—the baseline household survey (ENCASEH, 1997) and follow-up rounds (ENCEL, 1998--2000)—collected in 506 treatment and 320 control localities across seven Mexican states (Guerrero, Hidalgo, Michoacán, Puebla, Querétaro, San Luis Potosí, and Veracruz). Treatment localities began receiving \textit{Progresa} transfers in early 1998; control localities were enrolled in late 1999, providing an 18-month experimental window for comparison. The surveys collected individual-level information on labor supply, household composition, and income for all household members. Our mechanisms sample is restricted to elderly individuals aged 65 and older living in eligible households, identified using the program's eligibility criteria applied to the 1997 baseline. 

\subsection{Sample and Descriptive Statistics}\label{s:sample}

\p Our analysis sample is restricted to deaths among adults aged 65 and older, in highly marginalized municipalities classified as high (grade 4) or very high (grade 5) on the 1990 CONAPO Marginalization Index. We construct municipality-year cells combining death counts with population denominators and program variables. Table~\ref{t:descriptives} reports summary statistics comparing marginalized and non-marginalized municipalities in the pre-program period. As expected, marginalized municipalities have substantially worse socioeconomic conditions and much higher \textit{Progresa} enrollment rates.

\textcolor{red}{\p Our primary goal is to estimate the long-run mortality effects of \textit{Progresa} across the full universe of highly marginalized municipalities. The central identification challenge is that the 1999 enrollment snapshot diverges from actual coverage for municipalities enrolled after 1999: these localities were incorporated more gradually, and their intensity profiles by 2006 do not adequately reflect early-phase commitment. Our two-phase design resolves this by assigning $\textit{Intensity}_{2005}$ to the later wave of adopters, giving each group its own valid intensity measure and allowing us to include all highly marginalized municipalities without a phase-in restriction. \cite{parker2023conditional} document that after 2005 program expansion shifted substantially toward urban areas, making the two phases distinct and largely non-overlapping sources of intensity variation across municipalities, which supports the two-phase identification strategy. Appendix Figure~\ref{f:es_dd_sex_no_weight_app3} directly compares event study estimates across both sample definitions.\footnote{Figure~\ref{f:variation} in the appendix documents the spatial distribution of \textit{Progresa} intensity across all municipalities and separately for the highly marginalized subsample, confirming substantial variation in both early-phase (1999) and late-phase (2005) enrollment.} For the short-run replication of \cite{BarhamRowberry2013JDE}, we restrict to municipalities incorporated in 1998--1999, following their original design; $\textit{Intensity}_{1999}$ remains a valid proxy for actual coverage within the 1992--2002 window because program enrollment was stable and geographically concentrated through 2002.\footnote{Our sample differs from that used by \cite{BarhamRowberry2013JDE} in a way that is both conceptual and methodological. They restrict to municipalities incorporated into \textit{Progresa} in 1998 or 1999 because their identification strategy requires it: their intensity variable is continuous and time-varying, and the within-municipality variation that drives their estimates is only generated for municipalities that were actively being rolled out during their sample window (approximately 1990--2002). Municipalities incorporated after 1999 never accumulate sufficient lagged enrollment before their endpoint to contribute meaningful variation; including them would add noise without adding identification. The 1998--1999 window is thus not an arbitrary convenience sample but a requirement imposed by their estimator. Our approach does not share this requirement.}}

\subsection{Empirical Strategy}\label{subsec:empirical_strategy}

\textcolor{red}{\p We exploit variation in the proportion of households enrolled in \textit{Progresa} across municipalities before and after the program began in 1997.\footnote{\textit{Progresa} enrollment was originally assigned at the locality level, following a targeting algorithm based on community-level poverty scores \citep{ParkerTodd2017JEL}. Beneficiary counts are, however, available and used at the municipality level, which aggregates multiple localities. We follow \cite{parker2023conditional} in using municipality-level intensity because (i) the municipality is the finest geographic unit at which mortality data and population denominators are consistently observed across our 1991--2006 panel; (ii) within-municipality locality variation is absorbed by municipality fixed effects; and (iii) municipal coverage rates better capture population-level exposure. Staggered adoption estimators \citep{callaway2021difference, sun2021estimating, de2020two} require a clearly defined binary treatment date for each unit. Our setting does not satisfy this requirement: \textit{Progresa} was rolled out gradually within municipalities, and the intensity measure captures cumulative enrollment rather than a discrete entry date. We therefore follow the continuous intensity design of \cite{parker2023conditional} rather than a staggered binary treatment approach.} Following \cite{parker2023conditional}, who study the long-run effects of \textit{Progresa} on children's educational and labor market outcomes, we distinguish between municipalities that enrolled households more intensively in the early phase (1997--1999, denoted $\textit{Intensity}_{1999}$) and those that enrolled more intensively in the later phase (2000--2005, denoted $\textit{Intensity}_{2005}$). Parker and Vogl argue that after 2005 program expansion shifted substantially toward urban areas, making the two phases distinct and largely non-overlapping sources of intensity variation across municipalities. We adopt the same approach and apply it to elderly mortality.}

\p Because \textit{Progresa} was preferentially targeted toward more disadvantaged areas, a simple difference-in-differences comparing \textit{Progresa} municipalities to all others could be confounded by differential trends. Restricting the sample to highly marginalized municipalities mitigates this concern, as identification then requires parallel trends only between early and late roll-out municipalities within this group. Our estimates capture the impact of earlier versus later cumulative exposure to the program, under the assumption that additional years of access accumulate into mortality effects. We estimate:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:did}
\text{EMR}_{m,t}
= \beta_{0}\,\textit{Post}_{t}\times\textit{Intensity}_{m,1999}
+ \beta_{1}\,\textit{Post}_{t}\times\textit{Intensity}_{m,2005}
+ \delta_{m} + \gamma_{t} + \chi_{m,t} + \varepsilon_{m,t}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\text{EMR}_{m,t}$ is the Elder Mortality Rate (deaths per 1,000 inhabitants aged 65+) in municipality $m$ in year $t$. $\textit{Post}_{t}$ equals one for years 1997--2006 and zero for 1991--1996. Municipality fixed effects $\delta_{m}$ control for time-invariant unobservables; year fixed effects $\gamma_{t}$ absorb common time trends. $\chi_{m,t}$ is the Seguro Popular enrollment intensity, included in specifications that control for this potentially confounding program. Standard errors are clustered at the municipality level.\footnote{The EMR equals zero in a non-trivial share of municipality-year cells, particularly in small municipalities with few elderly residents. We address this in the appendix by estimating the same specification using raw death counts (Poisson regression) and log-transformed outcomes—both yield qualitatively similar results to the level specification. We retain the level outcome in the main analysis for three reasons: it eases interpretation (effects in deaths per 1,000), it preserves comparability across outcome definitions and with prior work, and it avoids discarding municipality-year cells with zero deaths that a log transformation would require. \cite{ChenRoth2024QJE} formalize the problems that arise when taking logs in the presence of zeros and recommend count-based alternatives; while Poisson regression addresses these concerns, we report it only as a robustness check to maintain comparability with the level specification throughout the paper.}

\p We also estimate event study specifications to trace dynamic effects and test for differential pre-trends:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:eventstudy}
\text{EMR}_{m,t}
= \sum_{k \neq 1996} \beta_{k}\,\mathbf{1}[t=k]\times\textit{Intensity}_{m,1999}
+ \sum_{k \neq 1996} \theta_{k}\,\mathbf{1}[t=k]\times\textit{Intensity}_{m,2005}
+ \delta_{m} + \gamma_{t} + \chi_{m,t} + \varepsilon_{m,t}
\end{equation}

\noindent with 1996 as the reference year. The $\beta_{k}$ coefficients trace the effect of early-phase enrollment intensity on mortality in each year relative to 1996.

\p Our interaction design, following \cite{parker2023conditional}, anchors identification to a discrete break in 1997 and explicitly controls for later-phase enrollment, separating the effects of the two rollout phases. The event study format of equation~(\ref{eq:eventstudy}) additionally allows us to test for differential pre-trends—a test.\footnote{Our specification also differs from \cite{BarhamRowberry2013JDE} in ways that matter for identification and robustness. Their baseline model regresses mortality on two-year lagged program intensity without interacting it with a post-program dummy, identifying off the contemporaneous spread of the program within a short window (approximately 1990--2000). This is a natural starting point but has two limitations: the choice of lag is arbitrary, and the absence of a pre/post interaction means the coefficient captures an average association across all years rather than a discrete shift at program onset. Additionally, time-varying lagged intensity design does not allow to test for pre-trends.} We include also include population weights because \textit{Progresa} targeted the most isolated and impoverished communities first, unweighted estimates may overstate the average effect by giving excess weight to the highest-intensity, smallest municipalities, whose mortality rates are also most volatile. Weighting by the elderly population gives each municipality influence proportional to its contribution to total elderly deaths.\footnote{\cite{BarhamRowberry2013JDE} estimates their model without population weights, implicitly giving equal influence to a municipality of 500 elderly residents and one with 5,000. In the highly marginalized sample, which spans a wide distribution of municipal sizes, this choice matters: small, remote municipalities—precisely those with the highest \textit{Progresa} penetration rates—are disproportionately influential in unweighted regressions. }

% \footnote{As shown in Appendix Table~\ref{t:did_robust_br2}, \cite{BarhamRowberry2013JDE}'s estimate shrinks by roughly two thirds when the two-year lag is replaced by a one- or three-year lag, underscoring its sensitivity to this choice.}

\p Our difference-in-differences coefficient $\beta_0$ in equation~(\ref{eq:did}) measures the average effect of a one-unit increase in early-phase enrollment intensity on the Elder Mortality Rate, and aligns with the population-level policy question: what was the average effect of \textit{Progresa} on the mortality of elderly Mexicans living in marginalized areas? Identification relies on the assumption that, conditional on municipality and year fixed effects, municipalities with higher $\textit{Intensity}_{1999}$ would have followed parallel mortality trends to those with lower intensity in the absence of the program.\footnote{Restricting to highly marginalized municipalities means we do not estimate a population Average Treatment Effect (ATE); rather, the coefficient is closer to an Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT) for the program's intended target population.}

\section{Results}\label{s:results}

\subsection{Main Results}\label{s:main_results}

\p Table~\ref{t:did} reports difference-in-differences estimates of the effect of \textit{Progresa} enrollment intensity on the EMR for adults aged 65 and older. The bottom rows of the table report the mean dependent variable and mean intensity values, providing the scale needed to interpret the coefficients. As documented in Figure~\ref{f:variation} and Figure~1, enrollment intensity varied substantially across municipalities—from near zero in the pre-program period to meaningful coverage by 1999 and further by 2005—with the geographic spread and temporal dynamics that motivate the two-phase design. Panel~A reports pooled results, Panel~B results for males, and Panel~C results for females. Within each panel, column~(1) presents unweighted estimates without Seguro Popular controls; column~(2) adds Seguro Popular intensity; column~(3) presents population-weighted estimates (weighted by the elderly population); and column~(4) combines weighting with Seguro Popular controls.

\p Across all specifications, columns, and panels, the estimated effect of \textit{Progresa} enrollment intensity on elderly mortality is small and statistically insignificant. The interaction of early-phase intensity ($\textit{Intensity}_{1999} \times \textit{Post}$) is close to zero in both the unweighted and weighted specifications, for the pooled sample and separately by sex. Adding Seguro Popular controls in columns~(2) and~(4) does not materially alter results. These findings hold regardless of whether we weight by the size of the elderly population.

\textcolor{red}{\p Figure~\ref{f:es_dd_sex_no_weight} presents the event study estimates for the weighted specification with Seguro Popular controls (column~4). The pre-program coefficients are close to zero and show no statistically significant differential trends, supporting the parallel trends assumption underlying our design. In the post-program period, some individual year-specific coefficients—particularly those associated with early-phase enrollment intensity in the years immediately following the 1997 rollout—are negative and individually significant, suggesting a possible transitory decline in elderly mortality in those years. However, these effects do not persist across the full post-program horizon: coefficients for later years revert toward zero, consistent with the null average DiD estimate documented in Table~\ref{t:did}. The pattern is broadly similar for males and females, and is not sensitive to the choice of reference year or the post-period window.}

\textcolor{red}{\p \textbf{Cause-of-death analysis.} We conduct cause-specific mortality analysis across eight causes—cancer, cardiovascular disease, respiratory disease, infectious disease, diabetes, nutritional causes, ill-defined causes, and accidents—for both our main highly marginalized sample and the Barham-Rowberry sample (municipalities incorporated in 1998--1999), over the full 1991--2006 horizon.\footnote{Appendix Figures~\ref{f:es_cod_br_a} and~\ref{f:es_cod_br_b} correspond most closely to the Barham and Rowberry (2013) analysis: same sample, similar period (1992--2002), event study format. Appendix Figures~\ref{f:es_cod_marg_a} and~\ref{f:es_cod_marg_b} extend these to the highly marginalized sample and the full 1991--2006 window.} Appendix Tables~\ref{t:did_robust_cod} and~\ref{t:did_robust_cod_br} present the corresponding DiD estimates. The event study results for cause-specific mortality in our long-run sample are notable: while the aggregate DiD estimate is not statistically significant, some individual year-specific coefficients for infectious disease and diabetes—the causes that \cite{BarhamRowberry2013JDE} identified as driving their short-run reduction, which they attributed to improved nutrition and preventive care visits \citep{BarhamRowberry2013JDE}—are negative and individually significant in the early post-program years. This pattern is consistent with a genuine but transitory short-run effect on these specific causes that faded as the broader disease environment in Mexico improved and the novelty of preventive care visits waned over the decade. It also reconciles our null aggregate long-run DiD with the positive short-run finding of \cite{BarhamRowberry2013JDE}: both can be true if program-induced health gains were real but did not persist.}

\textcolor{red}{\p Across all causes, both samples, and all specifications, the average DiD estimates show no statistically significant effects of \textit{Progresa} enrollment intensity on cause-specific elderly mortality. When considered jointly with the year-specific event study evidence above, this confirms that any transitory effect on infectious disease and diabetes mortality did not accumulate into a detectable average reduction over the decade. Appendix Figures~\ref{f:es_dd_sex_no_weight_app2} and~\ref{f:es_dd_sex_no_weight_app3} further confirm the robustness of our null average finding using the Age-Adjusted Mortality Rate (AAMR) as an alternative outcome and comparing unweighted against weighted specifications, across both samples.}

\subsection{Robustness}\label{s:robustness}

\textcolor{red}{\p We next turn to examine whether our long-run null is consistent with the short-run results in the prior literature. We replicate and extend the specification of Barham and Rowberry (\citeyear{BarhamRowberry2013JDE}, hereafter BR), who provided the first systematic evidence on \textit{Progresa}'s effects on elderly mortality, in three dimensions: a longer data window, a broader sample, and a sensitivity analysis across specifications. Their baseline model is:}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:br}
\text{EMR}_{m,t}
= \alpha_{0} + \alpha_{1}\,\textit{Intensity}_{m,t-2}
+ \delta_{m} + \gamma_{t} + \chi_{m,t} + \varepsilon_{m,t}
\end{equation}

\textcolor{red}{\p Appendix Table~\ref{t:did_robust_br} reproduces their exercise restricting to municipalities incorporated into \textit{Progresa} in 1998 or 1999. Row~(A) reports their original estimates; row~(B) shows our replication, yielding a similar negative and significant coefficient of approximately $-0.07$ deaths per 1,000 (Appendix Table~\ref{t:did_robust_br}, row~B, column~1). This close replication confirms that data and procedural differences are not the source of the divergence between our main results and theirs. Appendix Table~\ref{t:did_robust_br2} then extends the sensitivity analysis: adding population weights attenuates the estimate by roughly 50\%; using one- or three-year lags in place of the two-year lag further reduces it to approximately one third of the original magnitude. Statistical significance deteriorates substantially across these modifications because population weighting reduces the disproportionate influence of small, high-intensity municipalities—precisely those that drive the precision of the unweighted estimate—and because alternative lags generate smaller and noisier signals for the same underlying variation, jointly moving the estimated coefficient close enough to zero that standard errors span zero comfortably. Taken together, these results show that BR's finding, while internally consistent, is sensitive to specification choices that are each defensible on their own terms.}

\textcolor{red}{\p To put the magnitudes in perspective, we anchor the comparison to BR's own reported life expectancy gain. BR report a gain of approximately one year of life expectancy at age 65, based on their baseline specification \citep{BarhamRowberry2013JDE}. Our close replication of their specification on the same sample and window yields a coefficient of approximately $-0.07 \times$ (mean intensity) $\approx 1.6$ deaths per 1,000—roughly one quarter of BR's implied mortality reduction—which, using the same constant-hazard approximation ($\Delta e_{65} \approx \Delta \text{EMR} / \text{EMR}_0^2$), corresponds to a gain of approximately two to three months of life expectancy, with a 95\% confidence interval that comfortably includes zero.\footnote{Using a constant-hazard (exponential) survival approximation: $e_{65} = 1/\mu$ where $\mu$ is the annual mortality rate. Against a pre-program mean EMR of approximately 55 per 1,000 (Table~\ref{t:did}), the baseline life expectancy is $1/0.055 \approx 18.2$ years. BR's reported one-year gain implies a mortality reduction of approximately 3 per 1,000; our replication's $-1.6$ per 1,000 thus implies roughly $1.6/3 \approx 0.5$ years, or about six months. The two to three month range in the text reflects uncertainty across different aggregation assumptions. These calculations involve different samples and specifications—BR use municipalities incorporated in 1998--1999 without population weighting; we weight by elderly population—so the comparison should be interpreted with that caveat in mind.} Our long-run design, which extends to 2006 and covers all highly marginalized municipalities, detects no statistically significant effect in any specification, implying zero additional life years gained on average. The contrast between BR's one-year gain and our long-run null is consistent with a transitory short-run effect that did not persist, or with the sensitivity of the short-run estimate to specification choices documented in Appendix Table~\ref{t:did_robust_br2}.}



% \subsection{Threats to identification.} First, \textit{confounding by differential trends}: by restricting the sample to highly marginalized municipalities (grade 4 or 5), we compare early and late program adopters that shared the same poverty profile in 1990. Both groups were targeted by \textit{Progresa} from the outset; they differ only in the speed and intensity of enrollment. \subsection{Mechanisms: Labor Supply, Household Composition, and Income Effects}\label{s:mechanisms}

\p We take additional steps to examine the behavioral channels through which \textit{Progresa} could plausibly have affected elderly health. The theoretical case for expecting an effect rests on three program-specific pathways. The most direct is the \textit{health conditionality}: \textit{Progresa} required all household members, including the elderly, to attend one annual clinic visit, which could enable early detection of infectious disease, diabetes, and hypertension—precisely the causes BR identify as driving their short-run mortality reduction. \cite{BehrmanParker2013Demography} document that this conditionality increased health-service utilization among elderly women, and \cite{Aguila2024EHB} confirm that it improved hypertension diagnosis and treatment uptake, supporting the hypothesis that the clinical contact pathway was active. The second pathway is \textit{income and nutrition}: a 25\%-plus increase in household income could reduce nutritional deficiencies and improve access to medications and outpatient care for elderly members who share household resources. The third pathway operates through \textit{living arrangements}: if \textit{Progresa}'s transfers changed co-residence patterns—for instance, by reducing the need for adult children to migrate for work—elderly household members could benefit from increased informal caregiving and social support, both of which are associated with lower elderly mortality \citep{duflo2000child}. We test the second and third channels directly; the first is addressed by the evidence in \cite{Aguila2024EHB} and the cause-of-death analysis above.

\p Table~\ref{tab:combined_elderly} uses experimental data from eligible households in the original 1997 \textit{Progresa} rollout to examine the short-run behavioral responses in labor supply and household composition channels. Column~(1) reports the effect on weekly hours worked by elderly individuals; columns~(2)--(4) report effects on the probability of living alone, living with children, and living exclusively with other elderly members, respectively. The estimates show no significant changes: \textit{Progresa} did not reduce elderly labor hours and did not systematically alter whether elderly lived with children or alone. The null on living arrangements is informative—it rules out the informal-caregiving pathway as an operative mechanism, at least within the experimental sample and time horizon.

\textcolor{red}{\p Complementing the experimental evidence, we examine the income and nutrition channels using the National Household Income and Expenditure Survey (ENIGH), drawing on the mechanisms framework of \cite{Menares2026JHR} for the \textit{70 y Más} pension program. We estimate the effects of \textit{Progresa} intensity on elderly households' labor income, food consumption by category, and out-of-pocket health spending.\footnote{We estimate difference-in-differences models using ENIGH waves from 1992 to 2006, exploiting variation in \textit{Progresa} enrollment intensity across municipalities, following the same two-phase interaction design as in equation~(\ref{eq:did}). Outcome variables include individual labor income, household food expenditure by category (cereals, meat and dairy, vegetables, sugar and fats), and health spending (outpatient visits, medications, inpatient care).} All estimated coefficients are imprecise with large standard errors that preclude meaningful inference. This reflects the limited suitability of the ENIGH for this exercise: the survey over-samples urban households, elderly individuals constitute a small share of rural respondents, and few municipality-year cells contain enough elderly respondents to detect economically meaningful effects. These null ENIGH results do not contradict the experimental null from Table~\ref{tab:combined_elderly}—they reinforce it, providing convergent evidence from two independent data sources that the income, nutrition, and caregiving channels through which \textit{Progresa} might have reached elderly members were not activated in ways large enough to reduce mortality.}

\section{Conclusion}\label{s:conclusion}

\p This paper re-examines the medium-term effect of Mexico's \textit{Progresa} conditional cash transfer program on elderly mortality. Using fifteen years of vital statistics and administrative enrollment records, we find no evidence that \textit{Progresa} significantly reduced mortality among adults aged 65 and older during its first ten years of operation.

\p These null results emerge coherently from the evidence on mechanisms. As described in Section~\ref{s:mechanisms}, a CCT targeting women with children can plausibly reach the elderly through three channels: the annual health checkup conditionality, household income and nutrition effects, and changes in co-residence and informal care. The evidence suggests the first channel was partially activated—\cite{BehrmanParker2013Demography} document improved health utilization for elderly women and \cite{Aguila2024EHB} confirm higher hypertension diagnosis and treatment uptake—yet these gains did not translate into improved biomarkers or, as we show, lower mortality. The second and third channels show no activation at all: Table~\ref{tab:combined_elderly} finds no significant effects on elderly labor supply or living arrangements, and our ENIGH-based income and nutrition analysis is too imprecise to detect behavioral responses, likely because the survey was not designed to capture elderly subgroups in small rural municipalities. Taken together, the picture is of a program that nudges elderly household members toward the healthcare system once a year but does not produce the sustained changes in income, nutrition, or caregiving that would be needed to reduce mortality over a decade. The health checkup conditionality—one annual visit—is simply too minimal a clinical intervention to drive the mortality reductions that a pension transferring resources directly to older adults can achieve \citep{Menares2026JHR}.

\p We further show that the key prior finding of \cite{BarhamRowberry2013JDE} is sensitive to specification. Their result attenuates by 50\% with population weighting and shrinks further with alternative lag structures, losing statistical significance. Combined with our own estimates, we conclude that the evidence for large mortality effects of conditional cash transfers on older adults is not strong. In contrast, \cite{Menares2026JHR} find that Mexico's \textit{70 y Más} non-contributory pension—which transferred resources directly and unconditionally to adults aged 70 and older—did reduce elderly women elderly mortality in rural areas by 5.5\%, and \cite{MiglinoNavarrete2023AEJP} document a 2.7 percentage-point reduction in four-year mortality from Chile's non-contributory basic solidarity pension for the elderly. These contrasting findings point to a fundamental difference in how program benefits reach older adults.

\p When income arrives via a child-focused CCT, any gain to the elderly depends on the household's willingness and ability to redistribute resources across generations—resources that are in the first instance conditioned on children attending school. When income arrives as a direct, unconditional pension to the older adult, none of that intermediation is needed. The evidence suggests that households receiving \textit{Progresa} did not systematically redirect income toward elderly members' food, healthcare, or living arrangements, at least not enough to produce detectably lower mortality.
